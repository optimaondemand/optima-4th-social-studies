About This Unit

Unit 9 is a one-week pause to pull the middle part of Florida’s story together. In Units 7 and 8 your student followed Florida from a U.S. territory in 1821 to statehood, the Civil War, and Reconstruction, which ended in 1877. Now they put those events in order, find how each one led to the next, and make a claim about which event changed Florida the most.

The skill is claim, evidence, reasoning: make a claim you can prove, back it with facts you can check, and explain how the facts prove it. Your student first practiced pulling a story together in Unit 5, and they will keep using this move for the rest of the course.

“Historians don’t just describe what happened. They make claims about it and prove them with evidence.”From the reading in Lesson 4.09.02

The Crossroads at a Glance

Quick reference
Territory · 1821–1845
After the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819, Spain gave Florida to the United States. The Second Seminole War (1835–1842) was fought over Seminole land, and the Seminole Tribe of Florida is still here today.
Statehood · 1845–1861
Florida became the 27th state on March 3, 1845, as a slave state. Cotton plantations grew, and by 1860 nearly half of the people in Florida were enslaved.
Civil War · 1861–1865
Florida left the United States on January 10, 1861, and joined the Confederacy. The Battle of Olustee, the largest in Florida, was fought on February 20, 1864.
Reconstruction · 1865–1877
Freedom was announced in Tallahassee on May 20, 1865. Three amendments ended slavery, made citizenship and equal protection the law, and gave Black men the vote. In 1871 Josiah T. Walls became Florida’s first Black member of Congress.

The chain your student traces: Florida became a territory, then a state that allowed slavery. That led to secession and war. The Union’s win led to the amendments and to Black Floridians voting and holding office, and after 1877 many of those gains were taken back.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 9 is File 9, the second checkpoint: a door between Unit 8 and modern Florida. Like Unit 8, it is studied quietly, with no confetti or celebration.

Lesson 1
The Crossroads files are scattered. Your student sorts them into four eras and strings each cause to what it led to.
Lesson 2
Canvas asks for one more thing before modern Florida opens: a claim, defended with evidence.
Play along
Ask, “Did the checkpoint door open?” It’s an easy way into the lesson.

This Unit’s Virtues: Perseverance and Honesty

Each lesson has its own virtue.

Perseverance · Lesson 1
Sticking with hard work, even when there is a lot to remember. Your student reviews 56 years of Florida history and keeps going until every event has its place.
Honesty · Lesson 2
Following the evidence, even when it leads somewhere you didn’t expect. Claims rest on facts you can check, not on feelings.

The unit assessment asks about a third virtue, justice: your student names one person from Units 7 and 8 who treated people fairly even when it was hard.

  • 📜A source: a timeline of five real sources from Units 7 and 8, and a diagram of how seven events connect

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, a Historian’s Notebook synthesis or evidence-claim response, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 9 Assessment (about 35 minutes): 6 multiple-choice questions, two short questions about a reading on statehood, a claim with at least two pieces of evidence, and a reflection on justice.

What a strong claim looks like: one clear event (“I claim that statehood most changed Florida because…”), at least two specific facts from Units 7 and 8, and a sentence that explains how the facts prove the claim.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Both lessons · Hard history
The review returns to slavery, the Civil War, and Reconstruction, including the rights that were taken back after 1877. It adds nothing new or graphic, and the pages stay calm.
Lesson 4.09.02 · Judgment vs. evidence
Your student learns that “Slavery was wrong” is true but is a judgment, not evidence. Evidence is a fact you can check in a record. Holding both ideas is the point.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “What’s your evidence?” when your student makes a claim.
  • Ask “What did that lead to?” to help them link one event to the next.
  • Ask the questions in the Week 21 guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Let your student pick a different event than you would, as long as the evidence holds it up.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Choosing the “right” event for them. There isn’t one; the evidence is what counts.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
