Florida Becomes the 27th State
From territory to statehood — what changed and why it mattered

After Spain gave Florida to the United States in 1821, Florida became a U.S. territory: land that belongs to the United States but is not yet a state. The federal government (the national government in Washington, D.C.) was in charge. Floridians could not vote for president. Florida could send one delegate to Congress, the group that makes the nation's laws, but he could not vote there. Many settlers wanted more say in their own future.

The push for statehood. Through the 1830s, Florida's population grew fast. New settlers came from Georgia, the Carolinas, and other Southern states. Some built plantations, large farms worked by enslaved people. Others started businesses and cleared land for small farms. As more people came, more of them wanted statehood. In 1838, Florida held a constitutional convention, a meeting to write a state constitution, in St. Joseph (now Port St. Joe). A constitution is a written plan for how a government works.

The 1838 Constitution. The constitution set up a governor, a legislature (the group that makes a state's laws), and courts. But it also showed the deep unfairness of the time. It protected slavery, and it let only white men vote. Women, enslaved people, and free Black people could not vote. Statehood was not a story of freedom for everyone. Historians study both what the constitution created and who it left out.

Admission Day. On March 3, 1845, President John Tyler signed the law that admitted (let in) Florida as the 27th state of the Union, the United States. Florida came in as a slave state, a state where slavery was allowed. The same law also named Iowa, which would be a free state, where slavery was not allowed. (Iowa officially joined in 1846.) Pairing them kept the number of slave states and free states in Congress even. That balance became one of the great arguments that led toward the Civil War.

What statehood changed. Floridians could now vote for president, send voting members to Congress, and elect their own governor and legislature. Roads, ports, and new towns followed. But statehood also pulled Florida into the nation's fight over slavery, a fight that split the country apart 16 years later, when the Civil War began in 1861.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

In 1845, Florida became the 27th state in the United States. Statehood gave Floridians self-government (the power to run their own state) and voting members in Congress, but it also tied Florida to the nation's fight over slavery. A careful historian studies both what statehood created and who it left out.

🦉
🌴 Opti says…
Here's something to chew on: Florida's 1838 constitutional convention happened in St. Joseph, a small town on the Gulf Coast. That town was later destroyed by a hurricane and yellow fever. The building where Florida's future was written doesn't even exist anymore — but the document they created shaped everything that came next.

Word Roots
Latin Root: stat / stit — "to stand, to set up"

The Latin word stare means "to stand," and statuere means "to set up" or "to make stand." In 1845 Florida became a state, set up by its constitution.

🔎 Discovery Fact
Florida's capitol building in Tallahassee was finished in 1845, the same year Florida became a state. That building, now called the Old Capitol, still stands today as a museum.
