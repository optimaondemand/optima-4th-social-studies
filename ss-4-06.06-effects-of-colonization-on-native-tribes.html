🪶 Virtue Spotlight — Courtesy
Some history is hard to study, because real people were harmed. When we learn what happened to Florida's Native peoples after Europeans arrived, we owe them courtesy — treating their story with care, dignity, and respect. Courtesy here means using honest, evidence-based language. It means not turning real suffering into a game or a drama, and not skipping the hard parts just because they are uncomfortable. The Virtue Lens today is courtesy: how do we tell the truth about what happened, in a way that honors the people it happened to?

What Colonization Changed for Florida's Native Peoples
A careful look at real effects — in the order they unfolded

Long before Europeans arrived, many Native peoples lived in Florida — among them the Timucua in the north and center, the Apalachee in the panhandle, and the Calusa in the southwest. They had their own languages, beliefs, and ways of life. When Europeans colonized Florida (came, settled, and took control of land where Native peoples already lived), these communities faced serious and lasting changes. Read this timeline slowly, and notice how one event led to the next.

Effect 1 · New diseases spread. Europeans brought illnesses — such as smallpox and measles — that Native peoples had never been exposed to. Because their bodies had no protection against these new diseases, the sicknesses spread quickly and caused a great loss of life. This was the largest cause of death.

Effect 2 · The mission system changed daily life. Spanish missions (church towns run by Catholic priests called friars) were set up across Timucua and Apalachee homelands. Many Native peoples were pressured to change their religion, their work, and their daily routines. Under the labor draft, each mission town had to send men to work for the Spanish, farming fields and carrying heavy loads, often far from home. That left fewer hands to grow food for their own families.

Effect 3 · Conflict and slave raids broke communities apart. From 1702 to 1706, English soldiers from the Carolina colony and their Native allies, mostly Creek and Yamasee warriors, raided Spanish Florida. They burned mission towns and captured thousands of Native people to sell into slavery. In 1704 alone, more than 1,000 Apalachee were taken. Raiders reached the south, too. The Calusa had turned away Spanish missionaries, but raids and disease emptied their last towns in the early 1700s, and some survivors left for Cuba.

Effect 4 · Many people were displaced. Displaced means forced to leave your home. As their numbers fell and danger grew, many Native communities had to leave their homelands or join with other groups to survive. In 1704, many Apalachee burned their own mission towns and moved west. Most settled near Mobile, and their families later moved on to Louisiana.

The long result. Over many years, most of Florida's original tribes — the Timucua, the Apalachee, the Calusa, and others — nearly disappeared, and their ways of life were changed forever. This is a hard and sad part of history.

Why we study it carefully. Historians study this part of the past honestly, using evidence, and with respect for the real people it happened to. We do not skip the hard parts, and we never make light of real suffering. Telling the truth is how we honor these communities.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

The devastating pattern you're studying in Florida — disease, displacement, cultural destruction — played out across Texas with the same brutal results. Disease was the single largest killer in both states. Smallpox, measles, and influenza swept through Texas Indigenous communities that had no immunity, just as they did among the Timucua and Apalachee. The Karankawa along the Gulf coast, the Coahuiltecan peoples of South Texas, and communities throughout the mission system saw their numbers fall sharply. By the mid-1800s, the Karankawa, who had lived along the Texas coast for thousands of years, no longer lived as their own nation. Today, people who trace their families to them, such as the Karankawa Kadla, work to keep their history alive.

Texas fourth graders learn to see colonization's effects through multiple perspectives — the same skill you're building. From the Spanish perspective, missions were acts of faith and civilization. From the Indigenous perspective, missions meant losing everything: language, ceremonies, freedom, and often life itself. Some people entered missions voluntarily, seeking food during droughts or protection from enemies like the Apache and Comanche. Many resisted — the Karankawa fought Spanish settlement for decades. An honest account holds both realities: colonization built new communities and destroyed ancient ones, in Texas just as in Florida.

A gentle reminder from the archive: the descendants of Florida's first peoples (people born later in their families) are still here today. Some Apalachee families live in Louisiana as the Talimali Band of Apalachee. The Seminole and Miccosukee Tribes of Florida came later: their ancestors, mostly Creek people, moved into Florida in the 1700s, after many of the first tribes were gone. When we study the past honestly and with respect, we honor both the people who lived through it and the families who remember them.
