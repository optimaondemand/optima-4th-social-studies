Florida's Major Industries
How geography, resources, and people built the economy

An industry is a big kind of business, where many people do the same kind of work. Florida's economy (the way people earn and spend money) is built on its geography, the land, water and weather of a place. Florida has a warm climate (the usual weather year after year), a long coastline, rich soil and lots of natural beauty. These features drew industries that still shape the state today. Let's look at the big ones.

Tourism. Florida's number one industry is tourism, the business of visitors. Warm weather, beaches, theme parks and natural places brought a record 142.9 million visitors to Florida in 2024. Tourism creates jobs in hotels, restaurants, transportation and fun places to visit. It grew after Flagler and Plant built railroads and grand hotels in the late 1800s, and it never stopped growing.

Agriculture. Agriculture means farming and ranching. Florida is one of the top farming states in the country. The warm climate and long growing season help farmers grow citrus (oranges and grapefruit), sugarcane, tomatoes, strawberries and many other crops. For many years Florida grew more oranges than any other state. In the 1997–98 season, Florida picked a record 244 million boxes of oranges. Since then, a tree disease called citrus greening and strong hurricanes like Irma (2017) and Ian (2022) have hurt the groves. In 2024–25, Florida picked about 12 million boxes, and California now grows more oranges. Cattle ranching is also important, especially in central Florida.

Fishing and marine industries. Florida's long coastline supports a big commercial fishing industry, which means catching seafood to sell: shrimp, stone crab, lobster, grouper and snapper. Ports (towns where ships load and unload) like Jacksonville, Tampa and Miami handle billions of dollars in international trade, which means buying and selling with other countries. They connect Florida to Latin America, the Caribbean and the rest of the world.

Aerospace and defense. Aerospace is the business of building and flying aircraft and rockets. Rockets have launched from Cape Canaveral, on Florida's east coast, since 1950. NASA's Kennedy Space Center, right next to the Cape, has launched space missions since the 1960s, including Apollo 11 in 1969, the trip that took the first people to walk on the Moon. Why here? Rockets launch to the east, out over the Atlantic Ocean and away from people, and being far south gives them an extra push from the spinning Earth. Today, companies like SpaceX launch rockets from Florida too. Military bases across the state are part of the defense industry, which keeps the country safe. These industries bring jobs, research and new inventions.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Florida's major industries, tourism, agriculture, fishing, trade, and aerospace, are built on the state's geography: warm climate, long coastline, rich soil, and a location that's good for ships and rockets. Industries don't just make money. They shape how people live, work, and connect.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Florida's economy rests on tourism, citrus, and aerospace — and Texas has its own trio of powerhouse industries. After the Spindletop oil gusher in 1901, petroleum transformed Texas from an agricultural state into an energy giant. Cattle ranching — the state's oldest industry — still drives the economy across West Texas and the Panhandle. And like Florida's space coast at Cape Canaveral, Texas has NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, where Mission Control has guided every crewed American spaceflight since Gemini IV in 1965. Both states also share a massive tourism industry, though Texas draws visitors to the Alamo, Big Bend, and the San Antonio River Walk rather than beaches and theme parks.

The deeper lesson is that geography shapes what industries grow where. Florida's warm climate and coastline make tourism and citrus natural fits. Texas's underground oil reserves and vast grasslands make energy and ranching natural fits. Neither state chose its industries randomly — the land and climate pointed the way, and people built on what the geography offered. That's the connection between physical geography and economic activity that historians and geographers both study.

Word Roots
Greek Root: astro / aster — "star"

The Greek word astron means "star." You can find it in many English words as astro or aster. An astronaut is a "star sailor," a person who travels into space.

📖

astronaut — a person trained to travel into space · astronomy — the study of stars, planets and space · asteroid — a rocky object in space that travels around the Sun · disaster — a terrible event that causes great harm
