Today, watch for both — the bravery of the voyage, and what that voyage cost.

🔎 Discovery Fact
The navigator on Ponce de León's 1513 voyage was a pilot named Antón de Alaminos. Off the coast of Florida, his ships hit an ocean current so strong it pushed them backward, even with wind in their sails. Today we call that current the Gulf Stream.

Learn the Story
📖
Three Spanish Expeditions to Florida
1513 · 1528 · 1539

In 1513, a Spanish explorer named Juan Ponce de León sailed from Puerto Rico, a Spanish island in the Caribbean Sea. In the spring, his ships reached a green coast covered in plants and flowers. He named the land La Florida. In Spanish, that means "the flowery land." He chose the name partly because of the plants he saw, and partly because it was the Easter season, which Spaniards called Pascua Florida. Ponce de León was not looking for a magic Fountain of Youth. That story came later. He was looking for new land and valuable things he could claim for Spain.

Fifteen years later, in 1528, another Spanish expedition arrived. An expedition is a long trip made for a reason. This one was much bigger, and it was not ready for what it found. Pánfilo de Narváez sailed from Cuba and landed near Tampa Bay with about 400 men. About 300 of them marched north, looking for gold and food. Almost nothing went right. Storms, hunger, sickness, and fights with the Apalachee people ruined the expedition. Only four men lived through it. One of them, Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, later wrote a famous book about what happened.

Then in 1539, the biggest expedition of all arrived. Hernando de Soto sailed from Cuba and came ashore near Tampa Bay with more than 600 soldiers, more than 200 horses, and even pigs. Before this, de Soto had gotten rich in Peru, in South America, where the Spanish took gold from the Inca. He hoped to find rich cities like that here. He marched north through Florida and then all over what is now the southeastern United States. He never found those cities. His expedition spread disease, took food and supplies by force, and changed life for every Native nation it passed through. De Soto died near the Mississippi River in 1542 and never went back to Spain.

🔢

The labels. North is at the top. 1 · The title: “La Florida, made by Hieronymus Chaves.” That’s the Latin spelling of the mapmaker’s name. 2 · The small red buildings are Native towns. Historians think many of these town names came from men who marched with de Soto. 3 · “Baya de Spu Santo” means Bay of the Holy Spirit. That’s what de Soto’s men called the bay where they landed. Most historians think it’s Tampa Bay. 4 · “Martyres” is what Spanish sailors called the Florida Keys. 5 · “Canal de Bahama” is the Bahama Channel, the sea path Spanish ships sailed along Florida’s Atlantic coast.

👁️

What do I see? You don’t have to read the old writing. Use the numbered labels. Find the small red buildings (label 2). About how many can you count? Then find label 3. Which explorer’s men named that bay?

💭

What does it mean? This map was printed about 45 years after de Soto landed. The mapmaker filled the land with Native towns. What does that tell you about who was living in La Florida when the Spanish ships came?

⚖️

Why does it matter? This map shows both sides of first contact: Spanish names along the coast, and Native towns on the land. Why is a map like this useful to a historian? What can it show that a story about one explorer can’t?

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Three Spanish expeditions reached Florida between 1513 and 1539 — led by Ponce de León, Narváez, and de Soto. Their arrival was not a discovery; it was a meeting between strangers and the nations who had been living on this land for thousands of years.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

One of the explorers you just learned about — Cabeza de Vaca — is also one of the most important figures in Texas history. After the Narváez expedition disaster in 1528, Cabeza de Vaca and a handful of survivors washed ashore near present-day Galveston Island on the Texas Gulf coast. He spent the next eight years living among Indigenous peoples of Texas — the Karankawa and others — learning their languages, serving as a trader and healer, and walking thousands of miles across Texas and the Southwest before finally reaching a Spanish outpost in Mexico. His written account, La Relación, is one of the earliest European descriptions of Texas and its peoples.

Texas fourth graders also study two other major explorers: Francisco Vásquez de Coronado (1540–1542), whose expedition crossed the Texas Panhandle in 1541 searching for the legendary Seven Cities of Gold — cities that didn't exist — and René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle (1685), a French explorer who built Fort Saint Louis near Matagorda Bay. La Salle's colony failed completely, but it panicked Spain into strengthening its hold on Texas. The pattern is the same as in Florida: explorers came for gold, glory, and God — and their failures often mattered as much as their successes.
