About This Unit

In Unit 7 Florida became a U.S. territory. In Unit 8 it becomes a state, the 27th, on March 3, 1845. Your student then looks at life in Florida before the Civil War, when nearly half of the people in Florida were enslaved, follows Florida into the Civil War, and ends with Reconstruction (1865 to 1877), the years of rebuilding after the war.

This is the most serious unit of the year. The lessons tell the truth about slavery, war, and the rights that were won and then taken away, in a calm way and without graphic detail. Underneath it all is one question: when something changes, who gains, and who is left out?

“Winning rights on paper is only the beginning. Keeping them takes effort and courage, and people have to keep watch.”From the reading in Lesson 4.08.04

The Unit’s Timeline

Quick reference
  • 1838FloridaA convention at St. Joseph writes a state constitution. It protects slavery.
  • 1845U.S.March 3: Florida becomes the 27th state, as a slave state.
  • 1860FloridaNearly half of the people in Florida, about 62,000, are enslaved.
  • 1861ConfederacyJanuary 10: Florida secedes (leaves the United States) and joins the Confederacy.
  • 1864Civil WarFebruary 20: the Battle of Olustee, the largest battle fought in Florida.
  • 1865FreedomMay 20: freedom is announced in Tallahassee. The 13th Amendment ends slavery.
  • 1866PushbackIn 1865 and 1866, Florida passes Black Codes, laws that limit what freed people can do.
  • 1868U.S.The 14th Amendment. Florida’s new constitution creates public schools for all children.
  • 1870U.S.The 15th Amendment gives Black men the right to vote.
  • 1871FreedomJosiah T. Walls, born enslaved, becomes Florida’s first Black member of Congress.
  • 1877PushbackReconstruction ends. Jim Crow laws take back many of the gains.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 8 is File 8, and the teacher’s notes turn serious here. These files are studied quietly, with no confetti or celebration.

Week 19
A 27th star sewn onto the flag, an old scroll, the cotton belt, and storm clouds gathering.
Week 20
A real 1861 newspaper, a small museum of the war years, locked doors, and a brick wall. The assessment closes File 8.
Play along
Ask, “What did the teacher’s note say today?” It’s an easy way into the lesson.

This Unit’s Virtue: Courage

For a historian, courage means telling the truth about the past, even when it is hard to hear. Your student studies both the gains and the losses.

What it took
Enslaved people keeping their families and traditions alive, and escaping to Union lines. Black soldiers at Olustee. Freed Floridians voting, going to school, and holding office for the first time.
Who paid
The people who were enslaved. Soldiers and families on both sides of the war. Black Floridians whose new rights were attacked with threats and violence, and then taken away by law.

The unit assessment adds a second virtue, justice: your student picks one moment where justice was present or missing and explains why.

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each one follows the same four steps, so your student always knows what comes next:

  • 📜A real source: an 1846 map, a photo of plantation cabins, an 1894 battle print, an 1872 print of Black lawmakers

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, a Historian’s Notebook entry or Timeline Thread, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 8 Assessment (about 45 minutes): 8 multiple-choice questions, two short questions about a reading on Florida in 1860, two longer answers, and a reflection on justice.

What a strong answer looks like: your student explains what changed during Reconstruction and what happened when it ended, with specific dates and facts, and says why that pattern matters.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 19 · Slavery
Lesson 4.08.02 explains that enslaved people had no rights, could be sold away from their families, and were controlled by violence. It also shows how they kept their families and traditions alive and resisted. Calm and serious, nothing graphic.
Week 20 · War and backlash
An 1894 print shows soldiers fighting at Olustee, with some lying hurt; nothing is graphic. Lesson 4.08.04 explains that groups like the Ku Klux Klan used violence and threats to stop Black citizens from voting.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Who could vote? Who couldn’t?” at each step: statehood, the war, Reconstruction.
  • Ask “What led to that?” Every writing task this unit links one event to the next.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • If a lesson brings up hard feelings, talk about them. It’s okay for history to feel unfair.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Ending the story at 1865. The lessons ask students to see both the gains of Reconstruction and the rights that were taken back.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
