This Week at a Glance

A one-week review of Units 7 and 8. Your student looks back over 56 years, from 1821, when Florida became a U.S. territory, to 1877, when Reconstruction ended. In Lesson 1 they put the big events in order and find how each one led to the next. In Lesson 2 they choose the one event they think most changed Florida and defend that claim with evidence. There is no single right answer. What counts is the evidence. The week ends with the Unit 9 Assessment.

History skill
Claim, evidence, reasoning. Make a claim you can prove, back it with facts you can check, and explain how the facts prove it.
Virtues this week
Perseverance and honesty. Sticking with a big review, and following the evidence even when it leads somewhere you didn’t expect.

Good to know: These lessons look back at slavery, the Civil War, and Reconstruction, so the pages stay calm, with no celebrations. Nothing new or graphic is added. In Lesson 4.09.02 your student learns that “Slavery was wrong” is a true judgment but not evidence; evidence is a fact you can check in a record, like “By 1860, nearly half of the people in Florida were enslaved.” If your student asks why, that’s a great conversation to have.

4.09.01Lesson 1 Timeline Review: Territorial Florida to Reconstruction
Four eras, in order. Territory (1821–1845): after the Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819, Spain gave Florida to the United States, and the Second Seminole War (1835–1842) was fought over Seminole land. Statehood (1845–1861): Florida became the 27th state on March 3, 1845, as a slave state, and cotton plantations grew. Civil War (1861–1865): Florida left the United States on January 10, 1861, and the Battle of Olustee was fought on February 20, 1864. Reconstruction (1865–1877): freedom was announced in Tallahassee on May 20, 1865, three amendments changed the Constitution, and in 1871 Josiah T. Walls became Florida’s first Black member of Congress. Your student looks for how each event led to the next.

In the Florida Files

File 9 is the second checkpoint, a door between Unit 8 and modern Florida. The teacher can’t go through it until the class shows how the Crossroads files fit together. In Lesson 4.09.01 your student sorts nine event cards into four manila folders, one for each era. As they read, a live oak’s cut end grows one ring per era, oldest in the middle. They tap the numbers on a timeline of five real sources and tie red strings from each earlier event to a later one it led to. They bring sandhill cranes into line by matching words built on sequ (to follow), like sequence and consequence, and plan their writing on a three-step staircase. In Lesson 4.09.02 they sort statements into three trays (claim, evidence, opinion), then build an “argument house” as they read: facts for the ground, evidence for the pillars, reasoning for the beam, and the claim for the roof. They tap each event in the diagram to watch its arrows light up, pin their claim, and fill a claim card with their event, two facts, and how they’ll start their reasoning. A word game on clam/claim (claim, exclaim, proclaim, proclamation) ends with a real Florida moment: on May 20, 1865, the Emancipation Proclamation was read aloud from the steps of the Knott House in Tallahassee. Then the door opens, calmly, and modern Florida is next.

Words to Know

Use them at home!
eraa long stretch of time with something in common, like the Civil War era
sequencethe order things happen in (sequ = to follow)
consequencesomething that follows from, or happens because of, something else
claiman idea you can prove with evidence
evidencefacts you can check that show what really happened
opiniona feeling or belief that facts can’t prove
reasoningexplaining how your evidence proves your claim
proclaimto announce out loud, in public (clam = to call out)

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Can you put the four eras in order, with the year each one starts?Listen for: Territory, 1821. Statehood, 1845. Civil War, 1861. Reconstruction, 1865 (it ended in 1877).
  2. Name one event that led to another.Listen for: a real link, like “Florida joined as a slave state, and that led to secession in 1861,” or “the Union won, so the amendments ended slavery and gave Black men the vote.”
  3. What’s the difference between a claim, evidence, and an opinion?Listen for: a claim is an idea you can prove; evidence is a fact you can check; an opinion is a feeling.
  4. Which event do you think changed Florida the most? What’s your evidence?Listen for: one event and two specific facts (a date, a number, or a name). Any event works if the evidence holds it up.
  5. Why isn’t “Slavery was wrong” evidence?Listen for: it’s true, but it’s a judgment. Evidence is a fact you can check, like “nearly half of the people in Florida were enslaved in 1860.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Family timeline. Put four family events in order. Which one led to another?
  • Prove it. Make a claim about the best family dinner. Give two facts, not feelings.
  • Find the root. Claim, exclaim, and proclaim all hide a word part that means “to call out.” Then try sequence and consequence: what do they share?
