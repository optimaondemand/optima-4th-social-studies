An honest description of life in Florida 800 years ago doesn’t flatten the seasons into one long summer. Some months were hard. Some were generous. The honest historian notices both, and writes about a year the way the people who lived it would actually have felt it.

A Year in Florida, Long Ago
How the seasons shaped a life

When we say geography, we usually think about place — where something is on a map. But geography also includes time. The same piece of Florida land can feel like four different places during one year. The first peoples didn’t just live in Florida. They lived through every season of the Florida year, and the seasons shaped almost every choice they made.

The dry, cool season (roughly November to April) was a steady time. Less rain. Cooler nights. Mosquitoes calmed down. This was good weather for hunting deer, traveling between villages, fixing tools, building shelters, and trading with neighboring groups. For the Apalachee in the north, this was when fields were prepared and the next year’s planting was planned.

The wet, warm season (roughly May to October) was different. Heavy afternoon thunderstorms. Rivers and wetlands filled up. The Calusa paddled out in canoes to catch fish like mullet and snook and to gather oysters. The Apalachee planted corn, beans, and squash in fields fed by all that rain. Mosquitoes returned in big clouds — and people built and moved with that fact in mind.

Hurricane season (June to November, but most storms come August to October) was its own danger. The first peoples didn’t have radar, but they had centuries of knowledge — sky color, wind direction, animal behavior, and the rhythm of the year. A village ready for hurricanes had houses raised up on posts, food stored in sealed pots, and a plan. After a storm, families helped one another rebuild.

The brief, mild in-between weeks — the gentle days after one big season ended and before the next one started — were full of ceremonies, gatherings, and weddings. Old people taught children. Stories were told around fires. It was a time to rest.

Same place, four very different lives — all in one year. In Florida, geography was about when, not just where.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Geography is more than a place on a map — it’s also the time of year. Florida’s first peoples organized their daily lives around the dry, wet, hurricane, and mild seasons, so to describe their lives honestly, we have to look at the whole year, not just one season.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Florida's year-wheel shows four seasonal patterns shaped by water — wet season, dry season, hurricane season, and mild in-between weeks. Texas has its own seasonal rhythms, but they vary dramatically by region. On the Plains, the Lipan Apache and Comanche followed bison herds that moved with the seasons — north to summer grazing, south in winter — making the hunt itself a calendar. In the Southeastern forests, the Caddo's year was built around farming: clearing and planting in spring, tending fields through the rainy summer, harvesting corn in fall, and holding ceremonies and trade gatherings in winter when the harvest was stored. Along the Gulf coast, the Karankawa moved between inland camps in winter and coastal fishing camps in summer, following the food.

The lesson is the same one you're learning with Florida's year-wheel: geography includes time, not just place. Seasons told Indigenous peoples in both states when to plant, when to hunt, when to fish, when to travel, and when to gather for ceremonies. Whether it's an Apalachee farmer watching for spring rains in Florida's panhandle or a Caddo farmer doing the same in East Texas, the rhythm of daily life followed the rhythm of the land.

1. Which best describes Florida’s dry, cool season for the first peoples?
AThe hottest months with the heaviest rains.
BHurricane time — weather to hide from.
CA calmer time when hunting, traveling, building, and trading were easier.
Right — calm, dry weather made long jobs like hunting and traveling easier.
Think about what the words dry and cool would make easier. What jobs would you save for nicer weather?
2. The Apalachee planted big corn fields during the wet, warm season. Why does that make sense?
ABecause the soil is too cold in the wet season for anything to grow.
BBecause crops like corn, beans, and squash need warm temperatures and plenty of rain to grow.
CBecause nothing else was happening that season — they had to fill the time.
Exactly. Knowing when to plant is part of geography — the right season is part of the right place.
What do crops need to grow well? Heat plus what?
3. Someone claims Florida is "just one long beach day." What is the most honest response?
AThat’s not true. Florida has at least four very different seasons — dry/cool, wet/warm, hurricane, and mild — and each shaped daily life differently.
BFlorida’s weather doesn’t matter.
CAgree — every day is the same.
Yes — real facts about the seasons are the best way to show that idea is wrong.
Use the four seasons from today’s lesson. Real facts beat a catchy saying.
