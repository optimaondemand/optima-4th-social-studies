This Week at a Glance

Unit 8 ends. On January 10, 1861, Florida became the third state to secede (leave the United States) and joined the Confederacy. Your student learns what Florida gave the war (about 15,000 soldiers, plus beef, salt, and cotton), studies the Battle of Olustee (February 20, 1864), the largest battle fought in Florida, and learns how freedom was announced in Tallahassee on May 20, 1865. Then comes Reconstruction (1865 to 1877): new amendments, new rights for people who had been enslaved, and the people who pushed those rights back.

History skill
Checking a source. Ask who made it, when, and whether they were there. Then decide what to trust and what to check against other records.
Virtue this unit
Courage. Soldiers, families at home, and enslaved people who risked everything to reach freedom each showed a different kind of courage.

Good to know: Both lessons are serious, and the pages are calm, with no celebrations. Lesson 4.08.03 studies an 1894 color print of the Battle of Olustee that shows soldiers fighting, with some lying hurt on the ground; nothing is graphic. Lesson 4.08.04 explains that groups like the Ku Klux Klan used violence and threats to stop Black citizens from voting, and that laws later took back many of the rights Reconstruction had promised.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
4.08.03Lesson 3 Florida’s Involvement in the Civil War
On January 10, 1861, Florida’s leaders voted 62 to 7 to leave the United States, and Florida joined the Confederacy, the Southern states that wanted to keep slavery. Florida had the fewest people of any Confederate state, but about 15,000 Floridians served in the Confederate army, and Florida sent beef, salt, and cotton. On February 20, 1864, Confederate forces won the Battle of Olustee, near Lake City. Three of the Union army groups there were Black soldiers: the 54th Massachusetts and the 8th and 35th U.S. Colored Troops. Many enslaved Floridians escaped to Union lines, and some joined the Union army. On May 20, 1865, Union General Edward McCook read the Emancipation Proclamation from the steps of the Knott House in Tallahassee, and many Floridians still celebrate May 20 as Emancipation Day.
Sources: the New York Herald front page of January 13, 1861, and Kurz & Allison’s 1894 print of the Battle of OlusteeVirtue: CourageWord root: bell = war		 Historian’s Notebook entry (3–5 sentences): how Florida was involved in the Civil War, with at least two details (dates, battles, numbers, or facts about people).
4.08.04Lesson 4 Challenges During Reconstruction
Reconstruction (1865 to 1877) was the time of rebuilding after the war, when nearly 62,000 Floridians who had been enslaved were free. Three amendments changed the U.S. Constitution: the 13th (1865) ended slavery, the 14th (1868) made citizenship and equal protection the law, and the 15th (1870) gave Black men the right to vote. Formerly enslaved people in Florida voted, went to school, and held office for the first time. Florida’s 1868 constitution created public schools open to all children, and Jonathan Gibbs, a Black man, served as Florida’s Secretary of State and then as head of its public schools. Many white Floridians fought these changes with threats and violence, and by 1877 many of the gains had been rolled back.
Virtue: Courage		 Timeline Thread: two connected Reconstruction events, and how the first caused or shaped the second, using “because” or “as a result.”

In the Florida Files

File 8 finishes this week. In Lesson 4.08.03 your student reads a real newspaper front page from January 1861 and sorts what the paper tells us and what it leaves out. They walk through a small museum, opening five glass cases that each hold something real from the war years: Florida’s secession order, an 1862 magazine drawing of a salt works, the Olustee print, a poster that asked Black men to join the Union army, and the Knott House. They check the 1894 print like a historian (its caption has the wrong date), match war words by sending messages down a telegraph line, and plan their writing as a front page of their own. When the file opens, a calm card tells the story of Freedom Day. In Lesson 4.08.04 your student studies the Reconstruction amendments and the people who fought for and against new rights.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did Florida send to help the Confederacy?Listen for: about 15,000 soldiers, and beef, salt, and cotton.
  2. What happened at the Battle of Olustee?Listen for: February 20, 1864, near Lake City; the Confederates won; Black Union soldiers fought there with courage.
  3. Why should a historian check an old picture?Listen for: the Olustee print was made about 30 years later by artists who weren’t there, and its caption has the wrong date.
  4. What happened on May 20, 1865?Listen for: freedom was announced in Tallahassee, at the Knott House. Many Floridians still celebrate it as Emancipation Day.
  5. What did the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments do?Listen for: ended slavery; made citizenship and equal protection the law; gave Black men the right to vote.
