About This Unit

Unit 11 was about people who served Florida. Unit 12 is about the rules and choices behind a state that works. Over two weeks your student learns why Florida has a written constitution, how its three branches of government share power, which problems belong to a city, a county, or the state, and how careful citizens earn, save, spend, borrow, and handle risk.

The skill all unit long is finding proof in a real source: reading page 1 of Florida’s first constitution, following a real 1982 bill, spotting the words in a photo that prove whose job a flooded road is, and reading the warning box on a sample credit card bill.

“A plan only counts if it is written down, so everyone follows the same rules.”Page 1 of Canvas’s rulebook, Week 29

The Unit’s Quick Reference

Quick reference
  • 1838Florida’s first constitution is written at St. Joseph. It takes effect when Florida becomes a state in 1845.
  • 1968Voters approve today’s Florida constitution. A change, called an amendment, needs at least 60 out of every 100 votes.
  • 3Branches of government: legislative (makes laws), executive (carries them out), judicial (decides what laws mean).
  • 40 · 120 · 7State senators, state representatives, and Florida Supreme Court justices.
  • 1982Florida’s students vote for the Florida panther, and the Legislature makes it the official state animal.
  • 67Counties in Florida, plus more than 400 cities, towns, and villages. Each has a government of its own.
  • 2008Tropical Storm Fay floods Florida. City, county, and state crews each take care of their own roads.
  • $18.75 → $25What a 1940s savings-stamp album paid back after 10 years. Saving earns interest.

The thread your student follows: written rules, shared power, and careful choices all protect people, whether it is a whole state or one family’s budget.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history and helps her along. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 12 is File 12, and it happens at the door with 13 on it.

Week 29
The teacher finds Canvas’s rulebook, and your student’s pamphlet is printed on its first page. Three locks appear on the door: MAKE THE PLAN, CARRY IT OUT, CHECK IT’S FAIR. Your student opens them one branch at a time.
Week 30
One lock jams, and your student decides who should fix it. Then the door asks one last question, about money. Their answer slip goes in the slot, the EXIT sign lights, and the door starts to open.
What’s next
Unit 13 is the last file. Your student builds an exhibit from the whole year’s work.
The route
In Unit 11 your student voted on a way out. The teacher’s texts in Unit 12 still follow that choice.

Play along: ask, “What did you write on the answer slip for the door?”

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each lesson has four numbered tabs, so your student always knows what comes next:

1 · StartThe teacher’s texts, today’s question, the virtue, and a hands-on warm-up
2 · Story & sourceRead the history one part at a time and study a real source
3 · Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
4 · NotebookAnswer, write, and turn in
  • 📜A real source every lesson: the 1838 constitution page, photos of Florida’s government at work, FEMA photos from 2008, a 1942 school photo and stamp album, and the government’s sample credit card bill
  • 🔤A word root: scrib / script (write), jud (judge), solv / solut (loosen), and cred (believe, trust)
  • ❓3 evidence questions answered with details from the lesson
  • 📓One longer piece: a Historian’s Notebook entry (Lessons 1 and 3) or a Trade and Choice (Lessons 2 and 4)
  • ✏️Historian’s Sentence in your student’s own words
  • 📬Review & turn in at the end, ending with submitting in Canvas

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, a longer writing piece, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 12 Assessment (about 45 minutes, four parts): Florida’s constitution, the three branches of government, how citizens take part, and responsible choices with money.

What strong writing looks like: real details (the Senate’s 40 members, a county road, a $10 flat tire) and both sides of a choice: what you gain and what you give up.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 29 · Hard history
One label on the 1838 constitution page explains that “all freemen ... are equal” left out enslaved people, and that only white men could vote then. Today’s constitution says all people are equal before the law.
Week 30 · Money talk
The money lesson uses a made-up family and made-up numbers, and says so. It never asks about your own family’s money.
Week 30 · Not from Florida
The 1942 school photo is from New York City, and the credit card bill is the U.S. government’s sample. The lesson says so: schools all across the country sold the stamps, and every credit card bill has a box like that one.
Week 30 · The storm
The Tropical Storm Fay photos show flooded roads and repair crews. No one is hurt in them.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Who would fix that?” about a real problem near you: the city, the county, or the state?
  • Let your student plan a small amount of real money: some to spend, some to save, and what for.
  • Ask the questions in each week’s guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Look at the lesson’s real source together. What does it show for sure?

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Doing the hands-on parts for them. The games are how the lesson teaches.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
