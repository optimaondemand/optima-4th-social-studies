About This Unit

Unit 10 followed Florida from railroads to rockets. Unit 11 turns to the people. Over three weeks your student learns how Floridians worked for civil rights when the state was segregated, meets African American leaders who served Florida in many different ways, sees how immigrants shape Florida today, and learns how citizens solve problems with their local and state government, by voting, serving, and volunteering.

The skill all unit long is backing up an idea with evidence: checking guesses about a photo against the archive’s caption, comparing two sources, taking notes on a real meeting, and proving a leader’s virtue with a real event, date, or result.

“There are many ways to serve a community.”The big idea of Week 26

The Unit’s Timeline

Quick reference
  • 1871Josiah Walls, born enslaved, becomes Florida’s first Black member of Congress.
  • 1900In Jacksonville, about 500 students first sing “Lift Every Voice and Sing,” by James Weldon Johnson and his brother J. Rosamond Johnson.
  • 1904Mary McLeod Bethune opens a school in Daytona Beach with five girls, her son, and $1.50. It grows into Bethune-Cookman University.
  • 1920Women win the right to vote. Bethune helps Black women in Daytona register that fall.
  • 1956Two Florida A&M students are arrested on a Tallahassee bus. Black residents boycott the buses for about seven months.
  • 1960–64Sit-ins at lunch counters in Tallahassee and Jacksonville, marches in St. Augustine, and the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which made segregation illegal in public places.
  • 1967Florida’s Sunshine Law keeps most government meetings open to the public.
  • 2000The race for president is decided in Florida by just 537 votes.
  • 2004Hurricane Charley hits Punta Gorda. Public servants and volunteers help the town recover.
  • 2012The first members join the Florida Civil Rights Hall of Fame, including Bethune and Rev. C. K. Steele.

The thread your student follows: change came from many people working together over many years, and Floridians today keep building their communities by speaking up, voting, serving, and volunteering.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 11 is File 11, and it starts at the door with 13 on it.

Week 26
The teacher pushes on the door alone, and it won’t move. She learns it won’t open for one person alone. New name plates appear on her wall of names.
Week 27
Messages arrive from other rooms in Spanish, Haitian Creole, and Portuguese: she isn’t alone. She sends Canvas a polite request, and a stamp comes back: REQUEST RECEIVED.
Week 28
Everyone stuck inside votes on a way out, and your student breaks a tie. Then their leader profile prints onto a key card, and File 11 is closed.
What’s next
A sign appears: Canvas follows rules. Unit 12 is about Florida’s constitution and government, the rules of the state.

Play along: ask, “Which way did you vote to get out?” Later lessons remember your student’s choice.

This Unit’s Virtue

Every lesson in Unit 11 has the same virtue: courtesy. It looks a little different each week.

Week 26Courtesy & dignityTreating every person with dignity and respect, the very thing segregation took away.
Week 27Courtesy & respectRespecting people whose background is different from yours, and staying polite and clear when you ask for help or disagree.
Week 28Courtesy & serviceWaiting your turn, thanking people who serve, accepting a fair vote, and treating every person with dignity.

The unit assessment asks about courage: your student explains how one person or group from Unit 11 showed courage, and why courage matters for citizens.

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each lesson has four numbered tabs, so your student always knows what comes next:

1 · StartThe teacher’s texts, today’s question, the virtue, and a hands-on warm-up
2 · Story & sourceRead the history one part at a time and study a real source
3 · Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
4 · NotebookAnswer, write, and turn in
  • 📜A real source every lesson: photos from the State Archives of Florida, the Library of Congress, the National Archives, and FEMA
  • 🔤A word root: voc (voice), uni (one), lingu (tongue, language), civ/cit (citizen, city), vol (I want, I choose), and dign (worthy)
  • ❓3 evidence questions answered with details from the lesson
  • 📓One longer piece: a Historian’s Notebook entry (Lessons 1, 3, 5), a source study response (Lessons 2, 4), or a Leader Virtue Profile (Lesson 6)
  • ✏️Historian’s Sentence in your student’s own words
  • 📬Review & turn in at the end, ending with submitting in Canvas

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, a longer writing piece, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 11 Assessment (about 45 minutes): 8 multiple-choice questions, three short questions about the 1960 lunch counter sit-ins in Tallahassee, two longer answers (one leader’s challenge and why their actions mattered, and how citizens use voting, public service, and volunteering), and a reflection on courage.

What strong writing looks like: specific people, places, and dates (like the 1956 bus boycott in Tallahassee, or Bethune opening her school in 1904), and evidence that proves the point instead of an opinion.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 26 · Hard history
Segregation is taught calmly, with no graphic images. Lesson 1 tells, in one sentence, that the Moores died after a bomb exploded under their home on Christmas night in 1951, and how two college students were arrested on a Tallahassee bus in 1956.
Week 28 · Leaders were real people
Your student learns that leaders were not perfect. Walls lost his seat in Congress twice, Moore lost his teaching job, and Bethune sold sweet potato pies to keep her school going. Lesson 1 also mentions, in one sentence, that the Ku Klux Klan marched to scare Black women voters in Daytona in 1920, and that they voted anyway.
Week 27 · Good to know
Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens, not immigrants, because Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory. About 1 in 5 people in Florida was born in another country.
Weeks 27–28 · After the storms
Two lessons use the 2004 hurricanes in Southwest Florida to show government and volunteers at work. There are no storm-damage images.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” when your student names a leader’s virtue. The answer should be something the leader did.
  • Talk about a local problem together. Which office would you call: the city, the county, or the state?
  • Ask the questions in each week’s guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Look at the lesson’s real photo together. What does it show for sure? What can you guess?

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Doing the hands-on parts for them. The games are how the lesson teaches.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
