About This Unit

In Unit 4 Europeans reached Florida. In Unit 6 Spain stays. Your student learns why Spain came, how St. Augustine was founded in 1565, what life was like at the missions, and how about 200 years of Spanish rule changed Florida’s land, towns, and people. The unit ends by reading buildings and objects as evidence and organizing it all in a source notebook.

It’s the longest unit so far: four weeks and eight lessons. Through all of it, your student keeps the Timucua, Apalachee, Calusa, and other first peoples in the story, because Spanish Florida was built on their land.

“For about 200 years, Spanish rule changed Florida’s land, towns, and people, leaving effects we can still trace today.”The big idea of Lesson 4.06.05

Quick reference

  • 1564FranceFrench settlers build Fort Caroline near today’s Jacksonville.
  • 1565SpainMenéndez founds St. Augustine on Timucua land.
  • 1580sSpainMissions begin to spread across north Florida.
  • 1656ApalacheeMission San Luis becomes a town the Apalachee and the Spanish share.
  • 1668EnglandEnglish pirates burn much of St. Augustine.
  • 1672SpainWork begins on the stone Castillo de San Marcos (done 1695).
  • 1704ApalacheeEnglish and Creek raids destroy most missions; San Luis is burned and left.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 6 is File 6: a long, dusty hallway about Spanish Florida, where every finished lesson lights up a little more.

Week 13
A map file that won’t load, and a walled city with its “why it matters” grayed out.
Week 14
Mission records with the names scrambled, then daily-life scenes with no captions.
Week 15
Causes and effects split onto two walls, then a file she asks to open slowly.
Week 16
Buildings and objects with no labels, then the source notebook that opens the door.

Ask, “How much of the hallway is lit up now?” It’s an easy way into the lesson.

This Unit’s Virtues

Unit 6 uses a different virtue lens in most lessons. Each one is a question your student asks about the people in the story.

Week 13CourageWhose courage shows up at each step, on every side?
Week 14Service & PerseveranceWhom was each person serving? Who kept going, and at what cost?
Week 15Self-governance & CourtesySlow down and ask why. Tell hard stories with care.
Week 16HonestySay what the evidence shows, and what it can’t.

The unit assessment adds one more: justice. Was the way Native peoples were treated fair? What would justice have looked like?

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each one follows the same four steps, so your student always knows what comes next:

📍 LearnThe teacher’s message, the big question, and the virtue
🔍 ExploreRead the story and study a source
🧠 Check3 quick questions, not graded
📝 AssignmentAnswer, write, and turn in
  • 📅Timeline Thread: dates in order, so the story builds
  • 📜A real source: old maps, a fort, a rebuilt council house, a block of coquina
  • ❓2–3 evidence questions answered with details from the lesson
  • 📓One longer piece: a notebook entry or a timeline response
  • ✏️Historian’s Sentence: “I learned that ___ because ___.”
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, one longer writing piece, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 6 Assessment (about 45 minutes): multiple choice, a short reading on the Castillo de San Marcos, two longer written answers, and a reflection on justice.

What a strong answer looks like: your student explains the Spanish goals for the missions and the effects on Native peoples, uses specific names and dates, and says why understanding both sides matters.

💛

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Weeks 14–15 · Hard history
Forced labor at the missions, and the deaths of many thousands of Native people from disease, war, and slave raids. Lesson 4.06.06 is the most serious lesson of the unit. Nothing is graphic.
All unit · Still here
The lessons remind students that descendants of Florida’s Native peoples live here today, including the Seminole and Miccosukee tribes.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Whose land was this?” when a new town or mission comes up.
  • Ask “What’s your evidence?” and “What can’t this source tell us?”
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Let your student say how a hard lesson made them feel. Then help them find calm, respectful words for it.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Turning the hard parts into “that was just how things were.” The lessons ask students to weigh what was fair.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
