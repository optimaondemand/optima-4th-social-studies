Citizens in Action
Voting, serving, and volunteering in Florida

Voting is one of the most important things a citizen can do. A citizen is a person who belongs to a city, state, or country. When people vote, they choose the leaders who make decisions about schools, roads, parks, laws, and safety, even the leaders who plan for hurricanes. These leaders are called elected officials, because voters choose (elect) them. In Florida, you have to be at least 18 years old and registered to vote. To register means to sign up with your county's elections office. You can pre-register (sign up early) when you are 16, and you must be registered at least 29 days before an election. Elections happen at every level: local, state, and national. Close elections show that every vote matters. In 2000, the race for president was decided in Florida by just 537 votes.

Voting was not always open to everyone. Women across the United States won the right to vote in 1920, when the 19th Amendment (a change to the U.S. Constitution) became law. That fall, Mary McLeod Bethune helped Black women in Daytona sign up to vote. The night before the election, the Ku Klux Klan, a hate group, marched to scare them. The next day, they went to vote anyway. Florida's own lawmakers did not vote yes on the 19th Amendment until May 13, 1969, long after it was already the law. In 1957, Black and white citizens stood in one line to sign up to vote at Tallahassee City Hall. You can see them in the photo on this tab.

Some citizens serve their communities in public service jobs. Public service is work that helps the whole community, often for a city, a county, the state, or the country. When Hurricane Charley hit Punta Gorda on August 13, 2004, it was a Category 4 storm, one of the strongest kinds. Firefighters and police officers rushed to help people. Members of the Florida National Guard, part of the military, came to help too. Teachers help students learn and grow every day, and elected officials, from city council members to the governor, make decisions for the people who chose them. These jobs share one thing: the people who do them serve others.

Volunteerism is service without a paycheck. A volunteer is someone who helps because they choose to, not to get paid. After Hurricane Charley, volunteers came from all over. Some cooked hot meals in Punta Gorda. Some sorted donated clothes for families in North Port. Volunteers from AmeriCorps, a national service program, climbed onto damaged roofs and covered the holes with blue tarps (big plastic sheets). Across Florida, volunteers also pack meals at food banks, build homes with Habitat for Humanity, mentor (help and teach) younger students, plant trees, and clean up beaches and parks.

You don't have to wait until you're 18 to make a difference. Fourth graders can volunteer at school by helping younger students read, organizing supply drives, or helping with events. With a grown-up you trust, you can help in your neighborhood by picking up litter, welcoming new families, or collecting canned food for a food pantry. At 14, teens can join the Fire Explorer program at many Florida fire departments, like the volunteer in the 2005 photo. Small actions add up.

Voting, public service, and volunteering are how citizens take responsibility for the places they live. Taking responsibility means doing your part without waiting to be asked. A community where people show up, serve, and take part is a community that works. That's the kind of Florida these actions build.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Voting, public service, and volunteerism are important ways citizens take responsibility for their communities and make them stronger.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Texans take civic duty seriously — and the state has a unique history with voting and service. Texas was one of the last states to ratify the 19th Amendment granting women the right to vote, but Texas women had already won the right to vote in primary elections in 1918, making Texas the first state in the South to allow women's suffrage in any form. Today, Texas has a strong tradition of volunteerism — from disaster relief after hurricanes and floods to community food banks and Habitat for Humanity builds. After Hurricane Harvey in 2017, thousands of Texans organized rescue boats, donated supplies, and housed displaced families.

The connection to Florida is powerful: both states know that citizenship isn't just about voting — it's about showing up when your neighbors need you. Florida volunteers respond to hurricanes just like Texas volunteers do. In both states, public servants — teachers, firefighters, first responders — keep communities running every day. Whether you live in Tampa or Houston, the lesson is the same: a strong community depends on citizens who participate, serve, and take responsibility for the places they live.

Word Roots
Latin Root: vol — "I want, I choose"

The Latin word volo means "I want" or "I choose." The root vol is in words about choosing to do something because you want to, not because you have to.

📖

volunteer · a person who chooses to help without being paid · voluntary · done because you choose to, not because you have to · involuntary · done without choosing, like a sneeze or a hiccup · benevolent · wanting good things for other people; kind

🔎 Discovery Fact
Florida has a state agency with this root right in its name: Volunteer Florida. The Florida Legislature set it up in 1994. After hurricanes, it helps match volunteers and donated supplies with the places that need them most. Every volunteer chooses to help, and that choice is what vol is all about.
