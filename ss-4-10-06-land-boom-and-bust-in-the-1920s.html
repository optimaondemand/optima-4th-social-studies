⚓ Virtue Spotlight — Honesty
The land boom was fueled partly by dishonesty — sellers exaggerated, buyers didn't ask hard questions, and everyone pretended the prices would keep rising. Honesty means telling the truth even when a lie seems easier or more profitable. As you study today, notice what happens when honesty is missing from economic decisions.

Learn the Story
📖
Boom and Bust in 1920s Florida
When fast growth, big dreams and risky bets came crashing down

Easier to get to. By the early 1920s, Florida was already growing. Railroads reached all the way to Miami and Key West. Now many American families had something new: a car. New roads like the Dixie Highway let them drive from cities in the North and Midwest all the way to Miami. Many of them liked Florida’s warm winters and wanted a piece of Florida for themselves.

Selling sunshine. Land companies called developers (people who turn empty land into neighborhoods) bought big pieces of land, drew streets on them, and cut them into lots (small pieces of land, each big enough for one house). Then they sold the lots with ads, giant billboards and newspaper stories that promised sunshine, beaches and easy money. In 1924, Florida voters changed the state’s constitution (its main set of laws) so the state could not charge an income tax (money people pay the government from what they earn). Florida leaders hoped this would bring rich people to the state. Cities like Miami, Miami Beach, Coral Gables and Hollywood grew fast. Miami had 29,571 people in 1920. By 1930, it had 110,637, almost four times as many.

Buying to sell. Soon many people were buying lots not to live on, but to sell again for a higher price. This is called speculation. To hold a lot, a buyer could pay just a small part of the price, often 10 percent, as a deposit (a first payment). The paper that held the lot was called a binder. A much bigger payment was due about 30 days later. So many buyers raced to sell their binder to someone else for more money before that payment came due. People who did this were called “binder boys.” It worked only as long as new buyers kept coming and prices kept going up.

Warning signs. Not everyone was fooled. In January 1925, Forbes, a business magazine, warned that Florida land prices were high only because people expected to find someone else to buy the land, not because the land was worth that much. Then the boom began to jam. In October 1925, the railroads had so many freight cars (railroad cars that carry goods) stuck on their tracks that they stopped carrying anything except food, fuel and a few other needs, so builders could not get supplies. In January 1926, a big ship sank at the mouth of Miami’s harbor and blocked the way in for other ships. Fewer new buyers came. By the spring of 1926, prices had stopped going up and began to fall.

The bust. When prices stop going up, nobody wants to pay extra for a binder. People who held binders could not sell them, and many stopped making their payments. Some people had borrowed money to buy land and could not pay it back, and by 1926 some small banks began to close. Then, on September 18, 1926, a powerful hurricane hit Miami. The Red Cross counted 372 people killed and more than 6,000 hurt, and thousands of buildings were wrecked. The hurricane ended the boom. Building stopped. Many half-built buildings and empty lots sat for years. The land itself had not changed. It was the same sand by the same ocean. What changed was what people were willing to pay for it.

The lesson. Florida’s hard times started early. Three years later, in 1929, the U.S. stock market crashed, and the whole country fell into the Great Depression, a time when many people lost their jobs and their savings. Florida’s boom and bust teach a hard lesson: when prices are based on speculation instead of what something is really worth, the crash hurts the last buyers most. That is why honesty about risk (the chance of losing) matters, for sellers and for buyers.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Florida's 1920s land boom was driven by speculation: people buying land to sell again at a higher price, not to live on. When new buyers stopped coming in 1926 and a hurricane hit Miami that September, the bust wiped out fortunes and left half-built buildings empty. The boom and bust show what happens when prices are based on hype (big promises and excitement) instead of honest value.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Florida's 1920s land boom has a dramatic Texas parallel: the oil boom. After Spindletop blew in 1901, Texas saw wave after wave of oil speculation — land that cost pennies an acre suddenly sold for thousands. Towns like Ranger, Burkburnett, and Kilgore exploded overnight as wildcatters and speculators poured in. Just like Florida's "binder boys" who flipped land contracts, Texas had speculators buying and selling oil leases without ever drilling a well. And just like Florida's bust, the oil market crashed in the early 1930s when overproduction drove prices below the cost of pumping — until the Texas Railroad Commission stepped in to regulate output.

Both stories carry the same economic lesson: when speculation runs ahead of real value, a bust always follows. Florida's land speculators and Texas's oil speculators both believed prices would rise forever. Neither asked: "What is this actually worth?" That question — the difference between speculation and investment — is one of the most important ideas in economics. It's also why historians study booms and busts: they reveal what happens when a whole community stops thinking critically about risk.

Word Roots
Latin Root: spec — "to look"

The Latin word specere means "to look." You can find it in many English words as spec or spect. Today's big word, speculate, has it too. A land speculator was always looking ahead for a chance to sell for more.

🔎 Discovery Fact
The word speculate comes from the Latin speculari, "to keep watch," and specula, a lookout tower. In the 1920s, land speculators all over Florida were looking ahead, watching for a higher price.
