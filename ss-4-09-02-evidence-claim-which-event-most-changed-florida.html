Learn the Story
📖
Making an Evidence-Based Claim
How historians argue with facts, not opinions

Historians don't just describe what happened. They make claims about why events mattered. A claim is a statement you can defend (back up) with evidence. Today you'll practice this skill: choosing an event, making a claim about how important it was, and backing it up with facts.

What makes a strong claim? A strong claim is specific (it names exact events and dates), arguable (someone could disagree with it), and supported by evidence. "Statehood changed Florida" is too vague. "Statehood in 1845 changed Florida the most because Floridians could now elect their own governor, and Florida joined the nation's fight over slavery as a slave state" is specific, arguable, and uses evidence. (In 1845, only white men could vote.)

What counts as evidence? Evidence is a specific fact you can check: a date, a name, a number, or a detail from the lessons. "By 1860, nearly half of the people in Florida were enslaved" is evidence. "Slavery was wrong" is a judgment (what someone believes is right or wrong). It is true, but it is not evidence, because it is not a fact you can check in a record. Historians build arguments on evidence, not feelings.

The Claim-Evidence-Reasoning pattern. Here's a simple pattern for writing a strong claim:
• Claim: State which event most changed Florida and why.
• Evidence: Give at least two specific facts that support your claim.
• Reasoning: Explain how the evidence connects to your claim. Why do these facts prove your point?

Which event will you choose? You could argue for Florida becoming a territory (1821), the Seminole Wars, statehood (1845), the growth of slavery, secession (1861), the Civil War, or Reconstruction. There's no single right answer. What matters is how well you support your claim.

🤔

Pause & Think: Before you write, think: which event has the strongest evidence behind it? Which event caused the most other changes? That's often the strongest claim.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Historians make claims about why events mattered and support them with evidence. A strong claim is specific, arguable, and backed by facts. The Claim-Evidence-Reasoning pattern helps organize your thinking.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Texans debate a version of this same question: which event most changed Texas? Some argue it was independence from Mexico in 1836, when Texas became its own republic. Others point to the discovery of oil at Spindletop in 1901, which transformed Texas from a cattle-and-cotton state into an industrial powerhouse. Still others say the Civil War and Reconstruction reshaped Texas society more than anything else, ending slavery and rewriting the state's Constitution. Just like in Florida, there's no single "right answer" — what matters is how well a historian supports their claim with evidence.

Here's what makes this skill so important: in both Florida and Texas, the "most important event" depends on whose story you're telling. A rancher in 1890s Texas, a formerly enslaved person during Reconstruction, and an oil worker in 1901 would each give a completely different answer — and each would have real evidence to back it up. That's the heart of historical thinking: the evidence doesn't change, but the claims people make from it depend on what they value and whose experiences they center. A great historian considers all the perspectives before making their case.

Word Roots
Latin Root: clam / claim — "to call out"

The Latin word clamare means "to call out" or "to shout." You can find it in many English words as claim or clam. When you make a claim, you call out an idea and say you can back it up.

📖

claim — to say something is true, and be ready to back it up · exclaim — to call out suddenly, with strong feeling · proclaim — to announce something out loud to everyone · proclamation — an official announcement made to the public
