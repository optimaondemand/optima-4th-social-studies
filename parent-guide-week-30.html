This Week at a Glance

Unit 12 ends with two lessons about everyday choices. In Lesson 1 your student learns which problems belong to local government (a city or a county) and which belong to state government, and how a citizen brings a problem to the right place. In Lesson 2 they learn the big ideas of money: income, saving, spending, credit, and risk. The big idea: a careful citizen sizes up a problem before asking for help, and looks at both sides of a money choice before making it.

History skill
Finding proof in real sources. Your student finds the words in two real 2008 storm photos that prove whose job each road is, then compares a 1942 savings-stamp album with the U.S. government’s sample credit card bill.
Virtue this week
Service. Service means stepping up when your community needs help. It can also mean being careful with money, for your family and for your community.

Good to know: Lesson 2 uses one made-up family, the Riveras, and made-up numbers ($500 for the week) so the ideas are easy to follow. The lesson says so on the page. It does not ask your student about your own family’s money. The storm photos in Lesson 1 show flooded roads, with no one hurt.

4.12.03Lesson 1 Local and State Government Problem-Solving
Florida has one state government, 67 counties, and more than 400 cities, towns, and villages. A city is led by a mayor and a city council and takes care of city streets, streetlights, parks, police and fire departments, and clean water. A county is run by a county commission and takes care of county roads, libraries, and the sheriff’s office. Almost half of Floridians live outside any city, so the county is their local government. The state, led by the governor and the Legislature in Tallahassee, takes care of state highways, state parks, licenses, and the standards for what students learn. To know who to ask, size the problem: how many people does it touch? Citizens can speak at a meeting, write a letter, call, or sign a petition.
Sources: two FEMA photos by George Armstrong after Tropical Storm Fay, 2008: a State Trooper’s car on flooded State Road 46 near Geneva, and Leon County trucks repairing a county road in TallahasseeVirtue: ServiceWord root: solv / solut = loosen		 Historian’s Notebook entry (about 30 words or more): what local government takes care of, what state government takes care of, an example problem for each, and how a citizen could bring each one to the right place, plus a Historian’s Sentence.
4.12.04Lesson 2 Income, Saving, Spending, Credit, Risk, and Responsibility
Income is money you earn. Saving keeps money for later, and spending uses it now, so every choice has a trade-off. The next-best thing you give up is the opportunity cost. A budget is a plan for your money, with a little saved for emergencies. Credit is borrowing with a promise to pay back, usually with interest; a credit card is not free money. Risk is the chance of loss or harm. Surprises are a normal part of life, and people can accept a risk or protect themselves from it. Your student also meets three Florida entrepreneurs who took a risk to start a business: Vicente Martinez Ybor (cigar factories near Tampa, 1885), Abraham Lincoln Lewis (the Afro-American Life Insurance Company, Jacksonville, 1901), and George Jenkins (the first Publix, Winter Haven, 1930), and learns what Florida sells to the country and the world.

In the Florida Files

Last week the teacher opened all three locks on the door with 13, but the door stayed shut. In Lesson 4.12.03 the plan hits a snag: one lock slides shut again and jams. Your student sizes up real problems from Tropical Storm Fay, builds a county map one piece at a time, and sends city, county, and state road crews to the right roads. At the end they choose who should fix one lock on one door, the Help Desk for the hallway or the Main Office for all of Canvas, and a stamped repair ticket comes back: FIXED. In Lesson 4.12.04 the door lights up with one last question: “Can you make wise choices with money?” Your student deals out $20 in $5 bills, writes one family’s week into a check register, and follows a saver’s money and a borrower’s money for ten years. Their answer slip goes into the slot on the door. The EXIT sign lights up, and the door starts to open. File 12 is closed.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. A streetlight is out on a city street. Who should we tell?Listen for: the city. It is a small, close-to-home problem, so it goes to local government.
  2. How can you tell if a problem is for the city, the county, or the state?Listen for: size it. How many people does it touch? One neighborhood or city is local. Many counties, or all of Florida, is the state.
  3. You have $20. Why not spend all of it?Listen for: a surprise can come, like a flat bike tire. Saving is spending later, on a surprise or on something bigger.
  4. Is a credit card free money?Listen for: no. It is borrowed. You pay it back, plus interest if you pay slowly.
  5. Is taking a risk always a bad idea?Listen for: no. A risk can pay off, like starting a business. Think first, and protect yourself in case it doesn’t.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Size a problem. Spot one thing in your neighborhood that needs fixing. Is it a job for the city, the county, or the state?
  • Name the opportunity cost. Next time your student picks one treat over another, ask what they gave up.
  • Find the root. Credit and incredible share a part that means “believe.” What do solve and dissolve share?
