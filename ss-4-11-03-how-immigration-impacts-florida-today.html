A State Still Changing
How immigration shapes modern Florida

Florida is one of the most diverse states in the country. Diverse means made up of many different kinds of people. Immigration is a big reason why. Immigration is when people move to a new country to live there, and the people who do it are called immigrants. People come to Florida from the Caribbean (islands like Cuba and Haiti), Central and South America, and many other parts of the world. Some families arrived decades ago. Others came recently. Together, they've made Florida a place where dozens of languages are spoken and traditions from many countries live side by side.

Cuban Americans are one of the largest immigrant groups in Florida. Many settled in Miami, especially in the neighborhood called Little Havana. It is named for Havana, the capital of Cuba. There you can find Cuban coffee shops, domino parks (parks where neighbors play dominoes), and the famous Calle Ocho festival. (Calle Ocho is Spanish for “Eighth Street,” the neighborhood’s main street.) Haitian Americans built a busy, lively community in Little Haiti, with churches, restaurants, and markets where people speak Haitian Creole, a language from Haiti. Colombian, Venezuelan, Mexican, and Brazilian families have also made South Florida home in growing numbers.

Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens, not immigrants. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, a place that belongs to the United States but is not a state. So moving from Puerto Rico to Florida is like moving from one state to another. Many Puerto Rican families have moved to Florida, especially to the Orlando area. They have added to the culture, businesses, and community life of Central Florida. Together with immigrant families from many countries, they make Florida's population one of the most culturally rich in the nation.

Immigrants contribute to Florida's economy every day. To contribute means to give or add something. The economy is how a place makes, buys, and sells things, and how people earn money. Immigrants work in agriculture, which means farming: they pick citrus, tomatoes, and strawberries. They work in hospitals and clinics. They run restaurants, grocery stores, and construction companies (companies that build buildings and roads). Many Florida schools offer programs in English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole to serve families who speak different languages at home.

Immigration also shapes Florida's food, music, art, and festivals. From empanadas (filled pastries) and plantains (a fruit like a banana that is cooked) to reggae music from Jamaica and quinceañera celebrations (a big party when a girl turns 15), the cultural life of the state reflects the people who call it home. Learning about these contributions helps us understand the Florida we live in today.

💡 Key Concept

Immigration continues to shape Florida's population, culture, economy, schools, and communities in important ways every day.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

If Florida's story of immigration centers on the Caribbean and Latin America, Texas's story begins at the border. Texas shares nearly 1,300 miles of border with Mexico, and Mexican American communities have been part of Texas since before it was a state. Tejano culture — a blend of Mexican and Texan traditions — shapes everything from food (Tex-Mex cuisine) to music (conjunto and Tejano) to festivals like Diez y Seis de Septiembre, which celebrates Mexican independence. Cities like San Antonio, El Paso, and Laredo have deep roots in this shared heritage.

The bigger lesson is the same in both states: immigration doesn't just add people — it adds culture, labor, ideas, and identity. Florida's Little Havana and Texas's San Antonio River Walk both show how immigrant communities become central to a state's character. In both places, newcomers brought food, music, language, and traditions that shaped how everyone lives. Understanding immigration means understanding that every state's identity is built from the contributions of people who came from somewhere else.

Word Roots
Latin Root: lingu — "tongue, language"

The Latin word lingua means "tongue," and also "language," because we use our tongues to talk. The root lingu is in words about languages. In the word language, the root came to English through French, so it is spelled langu.

🔎 Discovery Fact
After English and Spanish, the language spoken most in Florida homes is Haitian Creole. More than 400,000 people in Florida speak it at home (U.S. Census Bureau). Many Florida kids are bilingual, and some are even multilingual. That's why some Florida schools teach in English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole.
