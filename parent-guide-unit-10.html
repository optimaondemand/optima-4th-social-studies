About This Unit

In Unit 9 your student pulled together Florida’s story from 1821 to 1877. Unit 10 opens the door to modern Florida. Over four weeks your student follows the state from a frontier of swamp and forest to a place of railroads, cigar factories, army camps, land sales, state parks, theme parks, and rocket launches.

The skill all unit long is cause and effect: a railroad, a war, a hurricane, or a new industry causes something else to change. Your student also practices reading a real source like a historian, sorting what it shows for sure from what it can only suggest, and asking “Where is the evidence?” when a story sounds too good.

“Courage isn’t always about battles. Sometimes it’s about believing a big idea can work.”From the virtue note in Lesson 4.10.01

The Unit’s Timeline

Quick reference
  • 1885Vicente Martinez Ybor builds Ybor City in Tampa. Cuban, Spanish, and Italian workers make it a cigar capital.
  • 1896Henry Flagler’s first passenger train reaches Miami, after Julia Tuttle offers him land. Miami becomes a city that July.
  • 1898About 30,000 soldiers gather in Tampa for the Spanish-American War. The battles are fought outside Florida.
  • 1904Mary McLeod Bethune opens a school in Daytona with $1.50 and five girls. It grows into Bethune-Cookman University.
  • 1912The first train reaches Key West, crossing the Florida Keys island to island on long bridges.
  • 1926A hurricane hits Miami on September 18 and ends the 1920s land boom. The Great Depression begins in 1929.
  • 1942World War II: training bases, shipyards, and enemy submarines off the coast. Towns dim their lights at night.
  • 1969Apollo 11 launches from Kennedy Space Center on July 16. Walt Disney World opens in 1971.

The chain your student traces: new railroads and roads brought people and towns. Warm weather, a long coast, and a place far south grew industries. Booms, a bust, hard years, and a war each changed how Floridians earned a living, and tourism and the space program brought new jobs after the war.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them, so each lesson she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 10 is File 10, the first file past the checkpoint door, and the room starts to change in every lesson.

Week 22
The teacher steps through the checkpoint door into modern Florida and hears a train whistle.
Week 23
A wall of blurry names comes into focus as your student learns about each person.
Week 24
A ship’s horn and a map with Tampa circled. Then shiny new doors pop up too fast, and half of them vanish.
Week 25
The lights dim, and a line of light shows under one door. A countdown clock stops at 3, the door shows 13, and File 10 is closed.

Play along: ask, “What changed in her room today?” It’s an easy way into the lesson. Door 13 matters later in the year.

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each lesson has four numbered tabs, so your student always knows what comes next:

1 · StartThe teacher’s texts, today’s question, the virtue, and a hands-on warm-up
2 · Story & sourceRead the history one part at a time and study a real source
3 · Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
4 · NotebookAnswer, write, and turn in
  • 📜A real source every lesson: photos and records from the Library of Congress, the National Archives, the State Archives of Florida, and NASA
  • 🔤A word root: like mot (to move), lect (to read), spec (to look), and tour (to turn)
  • ❓3 evidence questions answered with details from the lesson
  • 📓One longer piece: a Historian’s Notebook entry (Lessons 1, 3, 5, 7) or a source study response (Lessons 2, 4, 6, 8)
  • ✏️Historian’s Sentence in your student’s own words
  • 📬Review & turn in at the end, ending with submitting in Canvas

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, a Historian’s Notebook entry or source study response, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 10 Assessment (about 45 minutes): 8 multiple-choice questions, three short questions about a cigar factory in Ybor City, two longer answers (two people who helped Florida grow, and what boom, bust, and recovery teach about how economies change), and a reflection on perseverance.

What strong writing looks like: two specific details (like Flagler’s train reaching Key West in 1912, or Miami growing from 29,571 to 110,637 people in the 1920s) and cause-and-effect words: “because,” “this led to,” “so.”

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Weeks 24–25 · Hard history
A war, the 1926 Miami hurricane (the Red Cross counted 372 people killed), families losing jobs and homes in the Depression, and a 1942 submarine attack on a tanker seen from Jacksonville Beach. The pages tell these calmly, with no graphic images.
Stories vs. evidence
Your student learns that some famous stories aren’t proven: no one knows for sure what blew up the battleship Maine, and historians still question the story of Julia Tuttle mailing Flagler orange blossoms. They also learn that tourism started long before Disney, and that Apollo rockets launched from Florida, not Houston.
Week 22 · Up-to-date facts
Florida grew a record 244 million boxes of oranges in 1997–98, but disease and hurricanes cut the crop to about 12 million boxes in 2024–25. California now grows more. Tourism is Florida’s number one industry.
Week 25 · Real voices
Your student hears two lines from the U.S. Navy’s war diary for April 10, 1942, and NASA’s real recording of the Apollo 11 countdown.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “What did that lead to?” to help your student link a cause to its effects.
  • Ask “How do we know?” when a story sounds too good. It’s the question the unit practices.
  • Ask the questions in each week’s guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Look at the lesson’s real photo together. What does it show for sure? What can you guess?

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Doing the hands-on parts for them. The games are how the lesson teaches.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
