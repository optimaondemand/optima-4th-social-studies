This Week at a Glance

Last week your student met the people who built modern Florida. This week they study two big turning points. In Lesson 5 they find out why the U.S. Army gathered in Tampa in 1898 to fight the Spanish-American War in Cuba. In Lesson 6 they follow Florida’s 1920s land boom (a time when land prices climbed very fast) and the bust (the crash that came after). The big idea: Florida’s location and its land made it valuable, and it pays to ask what something is really worth.

History skill
Cause and effect, and what a source can prove. Your student checks what a photo shows, what only the records can tell us, and what a seller only promises.
Virtues this week
Courage and honesty. Soldiers who left home for a war far away, and buyers and sellers who should have told the truth about risk (the chance of losing).

Good to know: In February 1898 the U.S. battleship Maine blew up in Havana Harbor. Many Americans blamed Spain, but no one has ever proved what caused it, and many experts think it was an accident inside the ship. In Lesson 6, your student learns that a hurricane hit Miami on September 18, 1926, and the Red Cross counted 372 people killed. The boom had already stopped growing before the storm came.

4.10.05Lesson 5 Florida and the Spanish-American War
In 1898 the United States went to war with Spain, which ruled Cuba, an island just 90 miles south of Key West. The Army chose Tampa as its staging ground (the place an army gathers and gets ready) because it had a port and a railroad that ran right out to the ships at Port Tampa. About 30,000 soldiers came to a city that had 5,532 people in 1890, including the Rough Riders, led first by Leonard Wood and later by Theodore Roosevelt. Only one track ran the nine miles to the ships, so everything got tangled. The ships sailed in June 1898, and the battles were fought in Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines, not in Florida. Key West was a Navy base, and about 30,000 soldiers gathered at Camp Cuba Libre in Jacksonville. By 1900, Tampa had 15,839 people.
Source: a photo of troop ships in Tampa Bay, about 1898 (U.S. National Archives)Virtue: CourageWord root: mar = sea		 Historian’s Notebook entry (3–5 sentences): Florida’s role in the war, with two details and why it mattered, plus a Historian’s Sentence.
4.10.06Lesson 6 Land Boom and Bust in the 1920s
In the early 1920s, cars and new roads like the Dixie Highway brought people to Florida. Developers (people who turn empty land into neighborhoods) sold lots with ads and giant billboards, and in 1924 Florida voters changed the state constitution so the state could not charge an income tax. Many buyers wanted land only to sell it again for more, which is called speculation. A binder let them hold a lot with a small deposit, often 10 percent. Miami grew from 29,571 people in 1920 to 110,637 in 1930. Then the boom jammed: a business magazine warned in January 1925 that prices were too high, a railroad freight jam in October 1925 stopped building supplies, and a ship sank at the mouth of Miami’s harbor in January 1926. By spring 1926 prices had stopped rising, and the September 1926 hurricane ended the boom. Three years later, in 1929, the stock market crash began the Great Depression.
Source: a 1922 billboard selling lots near Miami (State Archives of Florida)Virtue: HonestyWord root: spec = to look		 Source study response (3–5 sentences): what caused the boom and what caused the bust, with two details, plus a Historian’s Sentence.

In the Florida Files

The teacher is still stuck inside Canvas, but the room keeps changing. In Lesson 4.10.05 a ship’s horn blows and a map appears on the wall with Tampa circled. Your student checks four Florida cities like a general would (a port, a railroad to the ships, a short trip to Cuba) and picks the one that gets the army. They punch an 1898 train ticket to read the story one stop at a time, fill in a ship’s manifest (its list of everything on board) using a real photo of troop ships, and hear Theodore Roosevelt’s own words about Tampa. They plan their writing as a telegram home. In Lesson 4.10.06 shiny new doors start popping up all over the room, too fast. Your student builds Miami’s skyline one reason at a time, then watches two bars, what people paid for land and what the land could really do, pull apart and snap back. On a real 1922 billboard they pick the honest question a careful buyer would ask about each promise, and half of the shiny doors vanish. They plan their writing by drawing one up-and-down line for the boom and the bust.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Why did the Army pick Tampa in 1898?Listen for: it had a port, a railroad that ran right out to the ships, and a short trip across the water to Cuba.
  2. Was the Spanish-American War fought in Florida?Listen for: no. Florida was where the army got ready; the battles were in Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines.
  3. What is speculation?Listen for: buying something, like land, not to use it but to sell it again for more money.
  4. Did the 1926 hurricane cause the bust all by itself?Listen for: no. Prices had already stopped rising after a railroad jam, a ship blocking Miami’s harbor, and fewer buyers; the hurricane ended the boom.
  5. If you saw a billboard promising “easy terms,” what would you ask?Listen for: an honest, careful question, like how much you would owe in all and when each payment is due.

What Gets Turned In

  • Each lesson: 3 evidence questions, a 3–5 sentence writing task, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas

Done well looks like: two specific details (like about 30,000 soldiers in Tampa, or Miami growing from 29,571 to 110,637 people) and cause-and-effect words: “because,” “this caused,” “this teaches us.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Trace the trip. On a map, put one finger on Tampa and one on Cuba. What would an army need to cross that water?
  • Ask the honest question. Look at one ad together. What does it promise? What would a careful buyer ask before paying?
  • Find the root. Marine, submarine, and mariner share a part that means “sea.” What do inspect, spectator, and spectacles share?
