This Week at a Glance

A new unit begins. In Lesson 1 your student learns how people in Florida worked for civil rights (the rights every citizen should have, like voting and fair treatment under the law) at a time when Florida was segregated, meaning Black and white people were kept apart by law. In Lesson 2 they meet five African American leaders who served Florida in different ways: a teacher, a songwriter, a union leader, a congressman, and a writer. The big idea: change came from many people working together, over many years, and there are many ways to serve a community.

History skill
Reading a photo like a historian. Your student makes guesses about a real photo first, then checks them against the archive’s own caption. In Lesson 2 they read the signs in a real photo from 1963.
Virtue this week
Courtesy. Treating every person with dignity and respect, the very thing segregation took away, and telling real people’s stories with care.

Good to know: Both lessons are taught calmly, with no graphic images. Lesson 1 tells, in one sentence, that Harry T. and Harriette V. Moore died after a bomb exploded under their home on Christmas night in 1951. It also tells how two college students were arrested on a Tallahassee bus in 1956. Lesson 2 says that James Weldon Johnson worked to stop mob violence against Black Americans.

4.11.01Lesson 1 Florida’s Role in the Civil Rights Movement
Under Jim Crow laws, Black Floridians had to use separate schools, restaurants, water fountains, and bus seats. Harry T. and Harriette V. Moore, teachers in Mims, led the NAACP (a group that works for equal rights) and helped thousands of Black citizens register to vote. In May 1956, two Florida A&M students, Wilhelmina Jakes and Carrie Patterson, were arrested after sitting in the only open seats, next to a white woman. Led by Reverend C. K. Steele and the Inter-Civic Council, Black residents boycotted the buses, walking and sharing rides for about seven months. In 1960 young people held sit-ins at lunch counters in Tallahassee and Jacksonville, and in 1963 and 1964 people marched in St. Augustine. Actions like these helped lead to the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which made segregation illegal in public places, and the work kept going after that.
Source: Reverend C. K. Steele and Reverend H. McNeal Harris sitting in the middle of a Tallahassee bus, December 24, 1956, the day the boycott ended (State Archives of Florida)Virtue: CourtesyWord root: voc = voice, to call		 Historian’s Notebook entry (about 25 words or more): one person or event, what they did, and how it helped create change, plus a Historian’s Sentence.
4.11.02Lesson 2 African American Community Leaders in Florida
Mary McLeod Bethune opened a school for Black girls in Daytona Beach in 1904 that grew into Bethune-Cookman University, and later advised President Franklin Roosevelt. James Weldon Johnson, principal of Stanton School in Jacksonville, wrote the words to “Lift Every Voice and Sing” in 1900; his brother J. Rosamond Johnson wrote the music, and about 500 students sang it first. A. Philip Randolph, born in Crescent City, started the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, a labor union, in 1925 and led the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. Josiah Walls, born enslaved, became Florida’s first Black member of Congress in 1871. Zora Neale Hurston grew up in Eatonville and wrote books and collected Florida folklore (old stories, songs, and sayings). Their work spans almost 100 years.
Source: leaders of the March on Washington, including A. Philip Randolph, August 28, 1963 (U.S. National Archives)Virtue: CourtesyWord root: uni = one		 Source Study (about 25 words or more): what the photo shows and how one or two leaders served Florida, plus a Historian’s Sentence.

In the Florida Files

The teacher is still stuck inside Canvas. Last week a door with the number 13 appeared. In Lesson 4.11.01 she pushes on that door by herself, and it won’t move. Your student guesses who two men on a 1956 bus are and seals the guesses in an envelope. They build a network of people across a map of Florida, from the Moores in Mims to the boycott in Tallahassee, the sit-ins, and St. Augustine. Then they open the envelope and check each guess against the State Archives’ caption, and plan their writing as a Florida historical marker. The teacher realizes the door won’t open for one person alone. In Lesson 4.11.02 new name plates appear on her wall of names. Your student fills a 500-voice choir to learn the story of “Lift Every Voice and Sing,” opens each leader’s gift (a school bell, a song, a union card, a vote, a book), lines the gifts up by year, reads the signs in a real 1963 photo, and plans a name plate for one leader. Five plates come clear, and one stays blank for leaders still to come.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What was the Tallahassee Bus Boycott?Listen for: in 1956, Black residents stayed off the city buses for about seven months to protest unfair seating rules. They walked and shared rides instead.
  2. Did one hero change the laws all alone?Listen for: no. Many people in many places worked together: students, churches, carpool drivers, voters, marchers.
  3. Did the Civil Rights Act of 1964 make everything fair right away?Listen for: no. It was a big step, and people kept working for years to make sure it was followed.
  4. Where was “Lift Every Voice and Sing” first sung?Listen for: at Stanton School in Jacksonville in 1900, by about 500 students.
  5. Pick one leader from Lesson 2. What was their gift to Florida?Listen for: a school (Bethune), a song (Johnson), a union and a march (Randolph), a voice in Congress (Walls), or books and folklore (Hurston).

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Guess, then check. Find an old family photo. Guess who, where, and when. Then ask someone who knows.
  • Many hands. Name one thing your family or neighborhood did together. Could one person have done it alone?
  • Find the root. Voice, vocal, and advocate share a part that means “voice.” What do union, unite, and uniform share?

See It in Florida

Optional
🏛️

In Mims, the Harry T. & Harriette V. Moore Cultural Complex stands where the Moores lived. In Tallahassee, the city bus station is named C. K. Steele Plaza. St. Augustine’s ACCORD Freedom Trail has markers about the marchers of 1963 and 1964. In Daytona Beach, Bethune’s home on the Bethune-Cookman University campus is a museum, and in Eatonville, The Hurston (the Zora Neale Hurston National Museum of Fine Arts) honors Zora Neale Hurston. Check dates and hours before you go.
