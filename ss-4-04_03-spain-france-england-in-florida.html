Word Roots
Latin Root: riv — "stream"

The Latin root riv comes from rivus, the Latin word for "stream." In Latin, rivalis meant people who used the same stream. Spain and France both wanted the same Florida coast, so they became rivals.

🔎 Discovery Fact
The French built Fort Caroline on a river, the St. Johns. The Spanish built St. Augustine about 35 miles south. Two rivals on the same coast, just like the old Latin idea of neighbors sharing one stream. Derive first meant to lead water away from a stream; now it means to get something from a source.

Learn the Story
📖
Three Powers, One Florida Coast
1564 · 1565 · 1586 · and beyond

For most of the 1500s, Spain had a claim on Florida. That means Spain said, “This land is ours,” even though the First Peoples already lived here. But Spain wasn't the only country with ships. France and England wanted Florida too — for the same reasons we studied yesterday: God, Gold, and Glory. When countries keep competing for the same thing, that's called a rivalry.

In 1564, French Protestants (a group of Christians) called Huguenots built Fort Caroline, a small fort and town, near what is now Jacksonville. They hoped to find a safe place to live and practice their faith. Nearby Timucua leaders, like Saturiwa, made friends with the French and traded with them. But Spain saw the French fort as a threat, a danger to Spain's claim. In September 1565, the Spanish admiral (a commander of navy ships) Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine. People have lived there ever since, so it is the oldest European town in the mainland United States. Less than two weeks later, Menéndez captured Fort Caroline. Then, at a place called Matanzas Inlet, his soldiers killed hundreds of French soldiers whose ships had wrecked in a storm (the Spanish word matanzas means "killings").

England joined the rivalry too. In 1586, the English captain Sir Francis Drake sailed into St. Augustine and burned much of the town. A quick surprise attack like this is called a raid. Spain rebuilt — but the warning was clear: any country with ships could try to take Florida.

For nearly 200 more years, Spain held on to Florida while England built thirteen colonies to the north. (A colony is a place ruled by a faraway country.) In 1763, after a long war, Spain had to give Florida to Great Britain. (By then, England had joined with Scotland to make one country, Great Britain.) The story of European Florida is not one flag's story. It is three.

The Calusa, Timucua, Apalachee, and Tequesta did not invite any of these powers. Some of their leaders made friends with one power to stand up to another. But they were the ones who lived through every battle, every fort, every flag change. Honest historians remember that.

Connect the Ideas
🗺️ Florida Files Note

This lesson's file was almost labeled "Spain's Florida Story" — but that's incomplete. The story of Florida's first European colonies involves Spain, France, and England. Using evidence, careful language, and the full picture keeps the record straight — and keeps the First Peoples in the story where they belong.

💡 Key Concept

Florida wasn't claimed by one European power — three countries fought over it. France built Fort Caroline in 1564, Spain founded St. Augustine in 1565, and Great Britain (England) took Florida in 1763. Through every flag change, the First Peoples of Florida were the only ones with deep roots in the land.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

The same three-power rivalry that shaped Florida played out in Texas — but with a different twist. In Florida, France built Fort Caroline (1564) and Spain responded by founding St. Augustine (1565). In Texas, France's move came later: La Salle built Fort Saint Louis on Matagorda Bay in 1685. The colony lasted only a few years — disease, conflict with the Karankawa, and La Salle's murder by his own men destroyed it. But Spain was so alarmed by a French flag on the Gulf coast that it launched multiple expeditions to find and destroy the fort, and then began building missions and presidios (military forts) across East Texas to block any future French claims.

The lesson is the same in both states: European powers didn't just explore — they competed. Spain, France, and England each wanted to control the Gulf coast and its resources. When one nation made a move, the others responded. Fort Caroline triggered St. Augustine. Fort Saint Louis triggered the Texas mission system. And through every flag change — in Florida and in Texas — Indigenous peoples were caught in the middle of a rivalry they never started, on land that had been theirs for thousands of years.
