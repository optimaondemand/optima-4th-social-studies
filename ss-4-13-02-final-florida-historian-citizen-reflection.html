Learn the Story
📖
The Journey Through Florida's Story
Reflecting on a year of history, evidence, and citizenship

You started this course as a student. You're finishing it as a Florida historian-citizen: someone who studies Florida's past and takes part in Florida today. That's more than a title. It means you know how to read a source (something from the past, like a letter, a map, or a photo), ask questions about evidence, and think carefully about what happened and why it matters. It means you can look at a map and see stories. It means you understand that history isn't just the past. It is what today is built on.

What you've learned. You studied Florida's land and water, its first peoples, the arrival of Europeans, life in Florida when Spain and then Britain ruled it as a colony (land ruled by a faraway country), the path to statehood, the Seminole Wars, the Civil War, Reconstruction (the years after the war when the country rebuilt), growth and change, the civil rights movement, modern Florida, government, the economy (money, jobs, and trade), and financial literacy (knowing how money works). Every unit added a new chapter and opened a new file.

What you've done. You studied sources: maps, documents, pictures, timelines, and artifacts (objects people made and used). Many were primary sources, made at the time they tell about, like a letter or a photo from that day. You looked at more than one perspective, which means one person's point of view. You answered evidence questions with specific details. You wrote Historian's Notebook entries. You solved glitches. You built an exhibit and six slides to show it. And through all of it, you helped your teacher find the way out of Canvas.

What comes next. The course is almost over, but being a historian-citizen doesn't stop. Every time you visit a Florida park, read a historical marker (a sign that tells what happened at a place), vote in a school election, or save money for something important, you're using what you learned this year. Florida's story keeps going, and you're part of it.

One last thing. Your teacher has been stuck inside Canvas all year. Every file you unlocked, every glitch you solved, every exhibit piece you built led here. Last time, the door with 13 swung open. Today, when you finish your reflection, your teacher steps out into the daylight, and a set of postcards arrives from St. Augustine, the Everglades, Tallahassee, Kennedy Space Center, and your own community. Your teacher is finally free.

What each number is. 1 · St. Augustine: "Fort Marion," a postcard from about 1901. Fort Marion is the name the United States gave the Castillo de San Marcos in 1825; the old Spanish name came back in 1942. Before 1907, the back of a postcard was only for the address, so the sender wrote a message on the front. · 2 · The Everglades: "Drainage Canal and Everglades, Miami," a postcard from the early 1900s. The building with the dome is the Capitol (today it is called the Old Capitol). · 4 · Kennedy Space Center: a NASA photo of the Artemis I rocket launching on November 16, 2022. · 5 · My Florida community: a blank card for the place where you live.

💡 Key Concept

A Florida historian-citizen knows the story, uses evidence, and serves the community by keeping history alive.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

A Texas historian-citizen carries the same responsibilities as a Florida historian-citizen: know the story, use evidence, and serve your community. In Texas, that means understanding how the state grew from six flags — Spain, France, Mexico, the Republic of Texas, the Confederacy, and the United States — into the diverse, complex state it is today. It means knowing that the Alamo is more than a building, that Juneteenth is more than a date, and that the oil under the ground changed not just Texas but the entire world's economy.

But here's what matters most as you finish this course: being a historian-citizen isn't about memorizing one state's facts — it's about carrying a set of skills and responsibilities wherever you go. You learned to read sources critically, build timelines, make evidence-based claims, understand how geography shapes history, and see how government and economics connect to daily life. Those skills work in Florida, in Texas, and in every state in between. You're not just a Florida historian-citizen or a Texas historian-citizen — you're a historian-citizen, period. And that's something worth carrying forward.

🌴 Opti, the team’s owl, says…
The word "reflection" comes from the Latin reflectere, meaning "to bend back." When you reflect, you bend your thinking back over everything you've experienced. A good historian doesn't just move forward — they pause, look back, and ask: what did I learn, and what will I carry with me?
🔤
Word Roots
Latin Root: flect / flex — "bend"

The Latin word flectere means "to bend." The root shows up as flect or flex in many English words. When you reflect, you bend your thinking back (re- means "back") over what you learned, the way a mirror bends light back to your eyes.

🔎 Discovery Fact
On July 16, 1969, Apollo 11 launched from Kennedy Space Center in Florida. On the Moon, the astronauts set down a panel of 100 small reflectors. Scientists on Earth still shine lasers at it today. The light bends back off the reflectors and returns to Earth, and the time it takes tells them how far away the Moon is. Florida's launch put a reflector on the Moon.
