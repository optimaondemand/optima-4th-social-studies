🪶 Virtue Spotlight — Honesty
A source notebook is built on honesty. When a historian writes down a source, a detail, and an explanation, they tell what the evidence actually shows — including the hard parts. Honesty means not making up missing details, not keeping only the happy parts, and not ignoring sources that are confusing. As you organize the early-Florida evidence today, the Virtue Lens is honesty: does my notebook tell the truth about what the sources really show?

Building an Early Florida Source Notebook
Source → Detail → Explanation: keeping the whole unit organized

This is the last file in the wing. Historians do not just collect facts — they organize them. A source notebook keeps evidence clear by writing three things for each piece of evidence: the SOURCE (where the information comes from), the DETAIL (one specific fact it shows), and the EXPLANATION (what that detail means and how it connects). Good notes help historians explain early Florida clearly and honestly.

SOURCE — where it comes from. A source might be an old map of Florida, a picture of a Spanish fort, or a mission record (a book the friars kept). Naming the source tells your reader how you know what you know.

DETAIL — one specific fact. A detail is something you can actually see or read in the source. For example: a map shows a town on the northeast coast; a fort drawing shows thick stone walls; a mission record lists the food people grew.

EXPLANATION — what it means. The explanation connects the detail to the bigger story. Thick fort walls explain that St. Augustine needed protection. A mission record explains how daily life changed for Native peoples.

Then put the events in time order. Early Florida has a clear chronology (the order of events in time). First, European explorers arrive, starting with Juan Ponce de León in 1513. Then St. Augustine is founded in 1565. Then missions like San Luis are built (San Luis in 1656). Spanish rule lasts until 1763 and has lasting effects on Native peoples.

And explain one cause and effect. A notebook lets you connect events: European arrival led to St. Augustine and the missions, which led to deep changes for Native peoples such as the Timucua and Apalachee.

Why honesty matters. Using evidence honestly means explaining what the sources actually show — including the hard parts. Historians do not keep only the happy parts, make up missing details, or ignore sources that are confusing. Honest notes tell the true story of early Florida.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

The Source-Detail-Explanation framework you're practicing works identically with Texas primary sources. A Texas example: Source: Cabeza de Vaca's La Relación (1542). Detail: He describes the Karankawa sharing food with the shipwrecked Spaniards and weeping at their suffering. Explanation: This challenges the common image of hostile Native peoples and shows that first contact often began with compassion, not conflict. Another: Source: Archaeological remains at Mission San José. Detail: Indigenous-style pottery found alongside Spanish ceramics in the same living quarters. Explanation: Even inside missions, Indigenous peoples maintained some traditional practices — colonization wasn't total erasure in every case.

The historian's skill you're building — reading a source, pulling a specific detail, and explaining what it reveals — is the same skill Texas fourth graders practice. And the hardest part is the same in both states: recognizing that most surviving sources were written by Europeans, not by the Indigenous peoples whose lives were most affected. When Cabeza de Vaca describes the Karankawa, he's giving us his perspective — valuable, but incomplete. A careful historian asks: whose voice is missing from this source, and what might they have said?

🌴 Opti says…
Here's a historian's trick: a good source notebook answers four little questions for every entry — who, when, where, and how do we know? If you can answer "how do we know," you've named your source. That last question is the one that keeps history honest.
