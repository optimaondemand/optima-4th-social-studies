About This Unit

People lived in Florida for thousands of years before any European ship arrived. In this unit your student meets four of those peoples, the Apalachee, Timucua, Calusa, and Tequesta, and learns how Florida’s land, water, and seasons shaped the way each one lived.

None of these peoples left written records, so your student works the way historians do: from pictures, artifacts, and careful thinking. By the end, they should be able to say who lived where, how the land shaped their lives, and how we know. They also learn to tell the difference between what a source shows and what we’re guessing.

“Where you lived in Florida shaped how you lived.”The big idea of Unit 3, from Lesson 4.03.01

The Four Peoples

Quick reference
Northwest panhandleApalacheeFarmers. Big fields of corn, beans, and squash in the hills near today’s Tallahassee.
North & centralTimucuaHunted deer, fished rivers, kept gardens. Round houses inside tall wooden fences.
Southwest coastCalusaLived from the sea. Built island towns on shell mounds. Powerful leaders, long canoe trips.
Southeast coastTequestaFish, sea turtles, and shellfish. Small villages near rivers, close to today’s Miami.

Native nations still live in Florida today, including the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida. Your student learns about them in the last lesson.

About the Story: The Florida Files

The whole course runs on one story. Your student’s teacher is stuck inside Canvas. She can find old Florida files in there, but she can’t open them. Each lesson, she sends a message asking for help. Your student does the history work, and the file unlocks. There are 13 files, one for each unit. Unit 3 is File 3.

Start of each lesson
A message from the teacher: what’s locked and what she needs.
End of each lesson
“File Unlocked” sums up what your student just proved.
New in Week 9
Phone texts and voice notes from the teacher right on the page.

Feel free to play along. Asking “Did you get her file open today?” is a great way to start a conversation about the lesson.

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons. Each one follows the same four steps, so your student always knows what comes next:

📍 LearnMeet the teacher’s message, the big question, and the virtue
🔍 ExploreRead the story and study a source
🧠 Check3 quick questions, not graded
📝 AssignmentAnswer, write, and turn in
  • 🏛️Virtue Spotlight: a virtue tied to the lesson (service, courtesy, honesty)
  • 📜A real source: a picture, an artifact, or old writing to study
  • ❓3 evidence questions answered with details from the lesson
  • 📓One longer piece: a notebook entry, a report, or a museum label
  • ✏️Historian’s Sentence: “Today I learned that ___ because ___.”
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Evidence questions, one longer writing piece, and a Historian’s Sentence, submitted in Canvas. The self-check in each lesson isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 3 Assessment (about 45 minutes): multiple choice, a short artifact task, two longer written answers, and a virtue reflection. Notes are allowed.

What “secure” looks like on the assessment: your student uses the right names and regions, separates what they see from what they guess, and backs up a comparison with specific examples.

💛

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 9 · Lesson 4.03.06
The lesson says, briefly, that many of Florida’s first peoples were lost after Europeans arrived, because of disease, war, and being forced off their land. Unit 4 picks up that story next.
All unit · Word choice
Lessons ask students to use each people’s real name instead of “Indians,” and to avoid calling Native life “simple.” You may hear your student correct a book or a TV show!

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” Every answer should point to a detail from the lesson or source.
  • Point them to the self-check and hint buttons before you step in.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Pull up a Florida map and find each people’s region together.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Adding facts from outside the lesson. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson’s sources.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
