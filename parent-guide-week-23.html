This Week at a Glance

Last week your student saw railroads and industries change Florida. This week they meet the people behind those changes. In Lesson 3 they learn how immigrants (people who move from one country to live in another) built communities that still shape Florida: Cuban, Spanish, and Italian cigar workers in Ybor City, Greek sponge divers in Tarpon Springs, and Bahamian and Minorcan families. In Lesson 4 they meet four individuals whose choices changed the state: Henry Flagler, Julia Tuttle, Mary McLeod Bethune, and Marjory Stoneman Douglas. The big idea: one person’s choice, or one family’s, can grow into something a whole state still has today.

History skill
Reading a source like a historian. Your student sorts what a photo or caption says, what they can infer (figure out from clues), and what they still question.
Virtues this week
Perseverance and self-governance. Immigrants who kept going in a new land, and people like Bethune who started fixing a problem without waiting for someone else.

Good to know: There is a famous story that Julia Tuttle mailed Henry Flagler fresh orange blossoms after the freezes of 1894–1895 to prove Miami stayed warm. Historians still question it. Your student practices asking, “Where is the evidence?” What is certain: Tuttle offered Flagler land, his first passenger train reached Miami on April 22, 1896, and Miami became a city that July.

4.10.03Lesson 3 Immigrant Contributions to Florida
In 1885, Vicente Martinez Ybor built Ybor City in Tampa. Cuban, Spanish, and Italian immigrants made it the cigar capital of the world, about 500 million cigars in 1929. The tobacco came by ship from Cuba. Workers paid a lector (a reader) to read newspapers and books aloud while they rolled cigars, and they chose what he read. In 1905, Greek divers came to Tarpon Springs and made it the sponge capital of the United States. Bahamians fished, sponged, and built in Key West and Miami, and Minorcans came in 1768 and settled in St. Augustine in 1777. Their foods and festivals blended with Florida’s older traditions.
Source: Lewis Hine’s 1909 photo of a reader in a Tampa cigar factory (Library of Congress)Virtue: PerseveranceWord root: lect / leg = to read		 Historian’s Notebook entry (3–5 sentences): how immigrants shaped Florida’s communities and culture, with two groups and one contribution, plus a Historian’s Sentence.
4.10.04Lesson 4 Significant Individuals in Florida’s Development
An individual is one person. Henry Flagler built railroads and hotels down the east coast to Key West by 1912, and Henry Plant did the same for Tampa. Julia Tuttle offered Flagler land to bring his railroad to Miami. Mary McLeod Bethune started a school for Black girls in Daytona in 1904 with $1.50 and five girls; she built desks from crates and sold pies to raise money, and the school became Bethune-Cookman University. Marjory Stoneman Douglas wrote The Everglades: River of Grass in 1947, the year Everglades National Park opened, and spent decades speaking up for the Everglades.
Source: a 1905 photo of Bethune with her students (State Archives of Florida)Virtue: Self-governanceWord root: duc / duct = to lead		 Source study response (3–5 sentences): how two individuals shaped Florida, what each did and why it mattered, plus a Historian’s Sentence.

In the Florida Files

The teacher is still stuck inside Canvas, but something is changing: each file your student finishes makes the room a little clearer. In Lesson 4.10.03 your student guesses who paid a cigar factory’s reader before hearing the caption photographer Lewis Hine wrote in 1909. They fill a family photo album, pasting in a keepsake to open each page (the Pérez family is made up, but every detail comes from real Ybor City history). Then, as a “caption detective,” they match pieces of Hine’s real caption to the numbered spots on his photo, sort out which words only the caption can tell us, and plan their writing as a Cuban sandwich. In Lesson 4.10.04 the teacher finds a wall of blurry names that come into focus as your student learns about each person. They help Bethune fill her school with only $1.50, plant each person’s choice in a garden and watch what it grew into, and stop to question the orange-blossom story. On the real 1905 photo they ink notes with three detective pens: blue for what the photo says, green for what they can infer, red for what they still wonder. They hear Bethune’s own words, “I leave you hope. I leave you a thirst for education,” and hang two Hall of Fame plaques before they write.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did a lector do in a Tampa cigar factory?Listen for: he read newspapers and books aloud while workers rolled cigars by hand; the workers paid him and chose what he read.
  2. Name an immigrant group and something they added to Florida.Listen for: Cubans, Spanish, and Italians and the cigar industry in Ybor City; Greek sponge divers in Tarpon Springs; Bahamians in Key West and Miami; Minorcans in St. Augustine.
  3. How did Mary McLeod Bethune start a school with only $1.50?Listen for: she made do, building desks from crates and selling pies to raise money, and her school grew into Bethune-Cookman University.
  4. Did Julia Tuttle really send Flagler orange blossoms?Listen for: nobody knows for sure, so historians ask where the evidence is. We do know she offered him land, and the train reached Miami in 1896.
  5. Pick one person you learned about. What would Florida be missing without them?Listen for: a choice and what it grew into, like Miami, a university, or a protected Everglades.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Family keepsake. Find one old photo or object in your home. What does it show for sure? What can you guess? What do you still wonder?
  • Make do. Like Bethune, find one thing at home you could reuse to make something new.
  • Find the root. Lecture, legible, and legend share a part that means “to read.” What do educate, conduct, and introduce share?

See It in Florida
