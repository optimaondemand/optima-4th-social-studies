As you study today, notice that courage shows up on every side of this story.

Word Roots
Latin Root: flor — "flower"

The Latin root flor comes from flos, the Latin word for "flower." Spanish grew out of Latin, so the Spanish word for flower is flor too. When Juan Ponce de León named this land La Florida in 1513, he was using that root. Florida means "flowery."

🔎 Discovery Fact
Florida's state flower is the orange blossom. Florida chose it in 1909. But orange trees didn't grow here before the Spanish came. Spanish settlers brought oranges to Florida in the 1500s, so even the state flower has a story that starts with Spanish ships.

Learn the Story
📖
Why Europeans Sailed for Florida
Three reasons, three expeditions, two sides of the story

In the early 1500s, Spain was one of the most powerful kingdoms in Europe. A kingdom is a country ruled by a king or queen. Spanish ships sailed across the Atlantic Ocean looking for new lands, new riches, and new converts. A convert is a person who changes to a new religion. Three reasons pulled them toward Florida.

1. Gold and riches. In the 1520s and 1530s, Spain took huge amounts of gold and silver from Mexico and Peru. Explorers hoped Florida had riches too, and stories spread about cities full of gold somewhere in North America. Many explorers risked their lives hoping to find one. Those stories turned out to be untrue, but the hope was powerful.

2. Land to claim for Spain. A claim is when a country says, “This land is ours.” European kingdoms raced each other to claim large pieces of the Americas. A captain who reached new land could claim it for Spain, even if people already lived there.

3. Spreading the Catholic faith. Catholic is one kind of Christian religion, and Spain was a Catholic kingdom. Many expeditions (big trips to explore new places) brought priests who wanted Native peoples to become Christian. Sometimes this was peaceful. Often it was forced.

The first three expeditions. Juan Ponce de León sailed from Puerto Rico and reached Florida's east coast in 1513. He named the land La Florida, which means “the flowery land” in Spanish. He chose the name partly because the land was green and full of plants, and partly because he arrived in the Easter season, which Spaniards called Pascua Florida. Pánfilo de Narváez arrived in 1528 with hundreds of men, but storms, hunger, and fighting destroyed the expedition. Hernando de Soto landed near Tampa Bay in 1539 and marched far inland with horses and soldiers in armor.

The other side of the story. Florida was already home. The Calusa, Tequesta, Apalachee, and Timucua peoples lived along Florida's coasts and rivers. They had towns, leaders, trade routes (paths people used to trade goods), and traditions that went back thousands of years. When Spanish ships arrived, they brought diseases like smallpox that Native peoples had never had before. Whole villages became sick. There was also fighting on both sides. A careful historian remembers both stories.

🔢

The labels. North is at the top. The words on this map are in Latin. 1 · The title box says a French artist, Jacques Le Moyne, made this map. He sailed to Florida with the French in 1564 and showed the map to the king of France. 2 · Spain’s coat of arms. A coat of arms is a royal symbol, like a badge for a country’s king. 3 · France’s coat of arms. Two countries put their symbols on one map, because both wanted Florida. 4 · “Sinus Ioannis Ponce” means Juan Ponce’s Bay. It is named for Juan Ponce de León, who sailed along this coast in 1513. 5 · “Hic descendit Pamphilus Narvaez” means “Here Narváez landed.” The mapmaker put the landing far up the coast. Historians today think Narváez landed near Tampa Bay, so this label is a mistake. 6 · “Montes Apalatci” means the Apalatci Mountains. The words say gold, silver, and copper are found there. Explorers never found mountains of gold in Florida. 7 · “Calos” is the Calusa, the people who lived on Florida’s southwest coast. Their name is on the map, but they didn’t make it.

What do I see? You don’t have to read the old Latin. Use the numbered labels. What is labeled? What is only there to look nice, like the ships, the sea monster, and the compass rose?

Why does it matter? Look at label 7. Whose voices are missing from this map? Where would a historian go to hear from the Calusa, Tequesta, Apalachee, or Timucua people who already lived here?

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Spanish explorers came to Florida looking for gold, land, and converts. Juan Ponce de León named it La Florida in 1513, but Florida was already home to the Calusa, Tequesta, Apalachee, and Timucua. A good historian tells both sides of this story.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

The explorers you're studying in Florida — Ponce de León, Narváez, de Soto — are part of the same wave that reached Texas. Cabeza de Vaca was actually on the Narváez expedition that landed near Tampa Bay in 1528. After the expedition collapsed, he and a handful of survivors washed ashore near present-day Galveston Island on the Texas Gulf coast. He spent eight years living among Indigenous peoples — the Karankawa and others — learning their languages and walking thousands of miles across Texas before reaching a Spanish outpost in Mexico. His written account, La Relación, is one of the earliest European descriptions of Texas and its peoples. The same man connects both states' exploration stories.

Texas fourth graders also study Francisco Vásquez de Coronado (1540), who marched through West Texas searching for the legendary Seven Cities of Gold — cities that didn't exist — and René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle (1685), who built Fort Saint Louis on Matagorda Bay. La Salle's colony failed completely, but it panicked Spain into strengthening its hold on Texas. The pattern is identical to Florida: explorers came for Gold, Glory, and God — and their failures often mattered as much as their successes.

Quick note from the archive: the word explore comes from the Latin explorare, meaning "to search out." Some word experts think it began as a hunters' word for a loud shout, like the shout hunters gave to drive animals out of hiding. Florida historians "explore" by calling out questions to the evidence, then listening carefully for what answers back.
