📖
Immigrants Who Built Florida
How people from many countries shaped communities, culture, and work

An immigrant is a person who moves from one country to live in another. Florida didn't grow on its own. People came from other countries and brought their skills, traditions (ways of doing things that families pass down), languages and dreams. These immigrants didn't just move to Florida. They helped build it. From cigar factories to sponge docks, from church festivals to neighborhood cafés, immigrant communities left a mark you can still see today.

Cuban immigrants and Ybor City. In 1885, a cigar maker named Vicente Martinez Ybor chose Tampa as the new home for his cigar business in Key West. He built Ybor City, a company town (a town a business builds for its workers, with houses, streets and shops). On April 13, 1886, the first Ybor City cigar was made. Thousands of Cuban workers followed, along with Spanish and Italian immigrants. Many of the Italians came from the island of Sicily in the late 1880s and 1890s.

Inside the cigar factories. Ybor City did not grow tobacco. Ships brought tobacco leaves from Cuba, and workers rolled them into cigars by hand. Ybor City became known as the cigar capital of the world. Its factories manufactured (made) hundreds of millions of cigars a year, about 500 million in 1929, their most ever. Workers brought Cuban coffee, Spanish bean soup and a tradition from Cuba: the lector, a person who sat up high and read newspapers and novels (long made-up stories) aloud while the workers rolled cigars. The workers paid the lector, and they chose what he read.

Greek immigrants in Tarpon Springs. In 1905, a Greek businessman named John Cocoris brought Greek sponge divers to Tarpon Springs on Florida's Gulf Coast. A sponge is a soft sea animal. Once it is dried, people use it for washing and cleaning. The Greek divers wore heavy diving suits and walked on the sea floor to gather sponges. Their skill turned the town into the sponge capital of the United States. Greek families also brought their Orthodox church (the kind of Christian church most people in Greece belong to), Greek foods like baklava (a sweet, flaky pastry with nuts and honey) and moussaka (a baked dish of eggplant and meat), and the yearly Epiphany celebration. Epiphany is a church holiday on January 6. A priest throws a cross into the water, and young divers race to bring it back. Thousands of visitors still come to watch.

Bahamian communities. Bahamian immigrants came from the Bahamas, a chain of islands just off Florida's east coast. They settled in Key West and Miami and worked in fishing, sponging and construction (building houses, roads and other things). They brought Caribbean cooking and music (from the islands of the Caribbean Sea and nearby), and boat-building skills.

Minorcan communities. Minorcans came to Florida even earlier. In 1768, more than 1,000 settlers, most of them from Minorca, an island of Spain in the Mediterranean Sea (the sea between Europe and Africa), came to work on a plantation (a very large farm) at New Smyrna. Life there was very hard. In 1777, the survivors moved about 70 miles north to St. Augustine. Their descendants (children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren) still live there. They keep traditions like pilau (a spiced rice dish) and Minorcan clam chowder, a soup made spicy with a small hot pepper called the datil. You can still order it in St. Augustine restaurants.

Why it matters. Immigrants didn't just add something new. They built industries, made neighborhoods and shaped the way Floridians eat, worship, celebrate and work. Florida's culture is a blend of many traditions, and that blending started with people who had the perseverance (they kept going when things were hard) to start over in a new place. The next time you eat Cuban bread, visit a Greek festival, or see a cigar shop in Ybor City, you're seeing immigrant contributions in action.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Immigrants from Cuba, Spain, Italy, Greece, the Bahamas, Minorca, and many other places helped build Florida's communities, industries, and culture. They brought skills, traditions, food, language, and faith, and Florida became stronger because of their perseverance and contributions.

🤠 Meanwhile in Texas…

Florida's immigrant communities — Cubans in Ybor City, Greeks in Tarpon Springs, Bahamians in Key West — have direct parallels in Texas. German immigrants settled the Texas Hill Country in the 1840s–1850s, founding towns like Fredericksburg and New Braunfels where German was spoken in schools and churches into the 1900s. Czech communities built dance halls and kolache bakeries across Central Texas. And just as Cuban cigar workers shaped Tampa's culture, Mexican and Tejano communities shaped San Antonio, El Paso, and the entire Rio Grande Valley — bringing food traditions, music, architecture, and Catholic mission culture that remain central to Texas identity today.

Both states prove the same point: immigrants don't just move to a place — they build it. The sponge docks of Tarpon Springs and the German biergartens of Fredericksburg aren't tourist curiosities. They're evidence that real communities brought real skills, traditions, and labor that shaped the economy and culture of their new home. When historians study immigration, they're studying how places become what they are — one family, one community, one generation at a time.

🌴 Opti says…
Here's a tasty twist: many historians say the Cuban sandwich Floridians know grew up in Ybor City's workers' cafés around 1900. It has ham, roast pork, Swiss cheese, pickles and mustard on Cuban bread. Tampa's version even adds Genoa salami from Italian neighbors. In 2012, Tampa named it the city's signature sandwich. One sandwich, many homelands!

Word Roots
Latin Root: lect / leg — "to read"

The Latin word legere means "to read." You can find it in many English words as lect or leg. A lector is "a reader," like the man who read aloud in Tampa's cigar factories.

📖

lector — a person who reads aloud to other people · lecture — a talk that teaches people about something · legible — clear enough to read · legend — an old story people have told for a long time

🔎 Discovery Fact
Tampa's cigar workers paid their lectors themselves, and they chose what the lector would read. After a long strike in 1931, the factory owners took the lectors out of Tampa's factories for good. In Cuba, some cigar factories still have a lector today.
