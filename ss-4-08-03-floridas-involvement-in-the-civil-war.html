📖
Florida in the Civil War
Secession, soldiers, supply lines, and the people who lived through it

On January 10, 1861, Florida became the third state to secede from the United States. To secede means to leave, or break away. Leaders at a meeting in Tallahassee voted 62 to 7 to leave. Florida then joined the Confederate States of America (also called the Confederacy), a new government formed by Southern states that wanted to keep slavery. The states that stayed in the United States were called the Union. The Civil War began in April 1861, when Confederate forces attacked Fort Sumter, a U.S. fort in South Carolina.

Florida's military role. Florida had fewer people than any other Confederate state, and few big battles were fought here. But Florida still played an important role. About 15,000 Floridians served in the Confederate army. Florida also sent the Confederacy beef, salt, and cotton. Salt mattered because it kept meat from spoiling in the days before refrigerators. At salt works along the coast, workers boiled seawater in big iron kettles until only the salt was left. After the Union took control of the Mississippi River in 1863, Florida's cattle became one of the most important sources of beef for Confederate armies.

The Battle of Olustee. The largest battle fought in Florida was the Battle of Olustee on February 20, 1864, near Lake City. Union troops marched west from Jacksonville, partly to cut off the beef and other supplies Florida sent to Confederate armies. Confederate forces defeated them, and the Union troops went back to Jacksonville. Three of the Union regiments (army groups of up to about 1,000 soldiers) were made up of Black soldiers: the 54th Massachusetts Infantry, the 8th U.S. Colored Troops, and the 35th U.S. Colored Troops. These soldiers fought with great courage. The battle showed that the war reached Florida, and that Black soldiers played an important part in the fight for the Union.

Life on the home front. The home front means life back home, away from the fighting. Families faced shortages (not enough) of food, medicine, and supplies. Women took on new jobs, like running farms, working in hospitals, and running businesses. Enslaved people watched the war closely. Many saw the Union as a path to freedom. When Union forces came near, many enslaved Floridians escaped to Union lines (places the Union army controlled). Some of them joined the Union army.

The end of the war. In April 1865, Confederate General Robert E. Lee surrendered (gave up) the main Confederate army in Virginia, and the war soon ended. In May, Union troops came to Tallahassee. On May 20, 1865, Union General Edward McCook read the Emancipation Proclamation (President Lincoln's order that freed enslaved people in the Confederate states) from the steps of a house in Tallahassee. Today it is called the Knott House. Freedom had come to Florida, and many Floridians still celebrate May 20 as Emancipation Day. The war cost many lives and hurt many communities. It ended slavery, but the fight for equal rights was far from over.

Source Study
The Battle of Olustee, as Artists Showed It in 1894
🖼️ "Battle of Olustee, Fla.," a chromolithograph (color print) copyrighted in 1894 by Kurz & Allison, 76 & 78 Wabash Ave., Chicago. State Archives of Florida, Florida Memory image N046635 (https://www.floridamemory.com/items/show/154548), via Wikimedia Commons (File:Battle of Olustee.jpg). Public domain. Kurz & Allison made a series of Civil War battle prints in the 1880s and 1890s; they were made to sell, about 30 years after the battles, and are known for historical mistakes. The caption reads: "Feby 26th 1864. Union: (Gen. Seymour) 8th U.S., 54th Mass., 1st N.C. Col'd T. Loss: 193 k'd, 1175 w'd, 460 miss'g." The battle was really on February 20, 1864; army reports list 203 Union soldiers killed, 1,152 wounded and 506 missing. The 1st North Carolina Colored Volunteers were renamed the 35th U.S. Colored Troops in February 1864. Six numbered labels: 1 the caption with the date, 2 the copyright line (1894, Chicago), 3 the U.S. flag, 4 a line of Black Union soldiers, 5 a Confederate flag far off on the hill, 6 pine trees.

Connect the Ideas
💡 Key Concept

Florida seceded from the Union in 1861 and joined the Confederacy. Florida sent about 15,000 soldiers and supplied the Confederacy with beef, salt, and cotton. The Battle of Olustee (1864) was Florida's largest battle. Black soldiers fought for the Union with extraordinary courage. The war ended slavery, but the fight for equality continued.

🦉
🌴 Opti says…
Did you know that Florida had fewer people than any other Confederate state? But after the Union took control of the Mississippi River in 1863, Florida became one of the most important sources of beef for Confederate armies. That made Florida's cattle ranches and salt works Union targets. In 1862, Union sailors burned a salt works on the Gulf coast. The war was fought in many ways: not just on battlefields, but through supply lines, salt works, and the work of the people who grew the food.

Word Roots
Latin Root: bell — "war"

The Latin word bellum means "war." You can find the root bel or bell inside words about fighting. Last lesson's word antebellum has it too: ante (before) + bellum (war).

📖

rebel — a person who fights against the people in charge · rebellion — a fight against the people in charge · belligerent — ready to fight or argue · antebellum — before the war; the years before the Civil War

🔎 Discovery Fact
During the Civil War, many people in the North called Confederate soldiers "rebels," because the Confederacy fought against the U.S. government. You can still visit an antebellum house in Tallahassee: the Knott House, built in the early 1840s. On May 20, 1865, just after the war, freedom was announced from its front steps.
