This Week at a Glance

Unit 12 begins with how Florida’s government is set up. In Lesson 1 your student learns why Florida has a written constitution, the written plan for how its government works, and why writing rules down protects everyone. In Lesson 2 they learn Florida’s three branches of government: the legislative branch makes the laws, the executive branch carries them out, and the judicial branch decides what laws mean. The big idea: Florida’s rules are written down so everyone can see them, and the power to govern is split three ways so no one person or group has too much.

History skill
Reading real sources. Your student studies page 1 of Florida’s first constitution, handwritten in 1838, and three real photos of Florida’s government at work, using numbered labels to find what each shows.
Virtues this week
Honesty and service. Honesty means doing things in the open, where everyone can see. Service means using your abilities to help others, like lawmakers, governors, and judges serving Florida.

Good to know: Lesson 1 explains, in one label on the 1838 page, that the words “all freemen ... are equal” left out enslaved people and that only white men could vote then; today’s constitution says all people are equal before the law. Lesson 2 has no hard history.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
4.12.01Lesson 1 Why Florida Has a Written Constitution
A constitution says how a government is set up, what it may do, and what rights the people have. Because it is written, everyone reads the same rules and leaders can’t change them in secret. Florida’s constitution opens with a Preamble (“We, the people of the State of Florida”), then the Declaration of Rights (freedoms like speech, religion, and a fair trial), then the three branches of government. Florida’s first constitution was written at St. Joseph in 1838 and took effect when Florida became a state in 1845; new ones followed in 1861, 1865, 1868, and 1885, and voters approved today’s in 1968. A change, called an amendment, goes on the ballot and needs at least 60 out of every 100 votes. The governor can’t change it alone.
Source: page 1 of the Florida Constitution of 1838, the handwritten copy, the only one known (State Archives of Florida, through the Library of Congress)Virtue: HonestyWord root: scrib / script = write		 Historian’s Notebook entry (about 25 words or more): what a constitution is, one part of Florida’s constitution and what it does, and why writing the rules down protects citizens, plus a Historian’s Sentence.
4.12.02Lesson 2 Branches and Functions of Florida Government
The legislative branch, Florida’s Legislature, makes the laws. It has two chambers: the Senate (40 senators) and the House of Representatives (120 representatives). A bill, an idea for a law, has to pass both. Then the executive branch, led by the governor, can sign it into law or veto it. The judicial branch, Florida’s courts up to the Florida Supreme Court and its 7 justices, decides what laws mean and can rule that a law breaks the constitution. Each branch can check another: two-thirds of both chambers can override a veto, the House can impeach a governor who breaks the law, and the governor picks higher-court judges from a list, then voters decide whether each justice stays. Your student follows a real bill: in 1982 Florida’s students voted for the Florida panther, and the Legislature made it the official state animal.
Sources: Florida House members taking their oath, 2014 (Florida House of Representatives); the governor signing a bill, 2013 (State Archives of Florida); the Florida Supreme Court building, 2019Virtue: ServiceWord root: jud = judge		 Trade and Choice (about 30 words or more): one branch, one choice it makes, the benefit, the cost or trade-off (what you give up to get something else), and what could happen without checks and balances, plus a Historian’s Sentence.

In the Florida Files

File 12 opens. Last week a sign told the teacher that Canvas follows rules, so in Lesson 4.12.01 she finds a thick book by the door with 13: Canvas’s rulebook. Your student plays a telephone game and watches one spoken rule change from room to room, while the written copy stays the same. They build the rulebook page by page and find rights from the real 1838 page that are still in Florida’s constitution today. Their citizen’s pamphlet is printed on page 1 of the rulebook, stamped WRITTEN DOWN, and three locks appear on the door. In Lesson 4.12.02 Canvas labels the locks: MAKE THE PLAN, CARRY IT OUT, CHECK IT’S FAIR. Your student carries the students’ 1982 panther bill through Florida’s government, opens the locks one branch at a time, and draws arrows showing how each branch checks the others. At the end the teacher turns all three locks in order. The door is unlocked, but it hasn’t opened yet.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Why does Florida write its rules down?Listen for: so everyone reads the same words, and leaders can’t change the rules in secret.
  2. Can the governor change Florida’s constitution?Listen for: no, not alone. A change goes on the ballot, and it needs at least 60 out of every 100 votes.
  3. What are the three branches, and what does each one do?Listen for: legislative makes the laws (the Senate and the House), executive carries them out (the governor), judicial decides what laws mean (the courts).
  4. If the governor vetoes a bill, is it gone forever?Listen for: not always. If two-thirds of the Senate and two-thirds of the House vote for it again, it becomes law anyway.
  5. How did the panther become Florida’s state animal?Listen for: students voted for it in 1982, and then the Legislature passed a bill that made it law.
